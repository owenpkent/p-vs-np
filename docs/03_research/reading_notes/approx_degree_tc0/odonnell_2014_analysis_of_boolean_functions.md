## Summary

This is the standard graduate textbook on analysis of Boolean functions: the study of $f : \{-1,1\}^n \to \{-1,1\}$ (or $\to \mathbb{R}$) via the unique multilinear "Fourier expansion" $f = \sum_{S \subseteq [n]} \widehat f(S) \chi_S$ over the parity-character basis. Its core contribution as a reference is a uniform vocabulary and toolkit (Fourier coefficients, degree, influences, total influence, noise stability / sensitivity, the noise operator $\mathrm{T}_\rho$, spectral concentration, restrictions) and a set of "highlight" applications threading that toolkit through property testing, social choice, learning, pseudorandomness, circuit complexity, and hardness of approximation. For this project the load-bearing chapters are 1 through 5: they fix the definitions an approximate-degree / polynomial-method survey must speak, and they contain the polynomial-method-adjacent results (PTF sparsity lower bounds, the Majority Fourier asymptotics, the degree-1 weight theorems, the Switching Lemma and LMN concentration for $\mathsf{AC}^0$).

## Key definitions and results

### Chapter 1: Boolean functions and the Fourier expansion (read in full)

- **Fourier expansion (Theorem 1.1, section 1.2, p.22).** Every $f : \{-1,1\}^n \to \mathbb{R}$ is uniquely expressible as a multilinear polynomial $f(x) = \sum_{S \subseteq [n]} \widehat f(S)\, x^S$ where $x^S = \prod_{i \in S} x_i$ (with $x^\emptyset = 1$). The coefficients $\widehat f(S)$ are the Fourier coefficients; collectively the Fourier spectrum. Worked examples: $\max_2(x) = \tfrac12 + \tfrac12 x_1 + \tfrac12 x_2 - \tfrac12 x_1 x_2$ (eq. 1.1) and $\mathrm{Maj}_3(x) = \tfrac12 x_1 + \tfrac12 x_2 + \tfrac12 x_3 - \tfrac12 x_1 x_2 x_3$ (eq. 1.2). Derivation is by interpolation with indicator polynomials $1_{\{a\}}(x) = \prod_i \tfrac{1 + a_i x_i}{2}$.
- **Parity characters and orthonormality (section 1.3, Theorem 1.5, p.24).** $\chi_S(x) = \prod_{i \in S} x_i$ are exactly the parity (XOR) functions. Over $\mathbb{F}_2^n$, $\chi_S(x) = (-1)^{\sum_{i\in S} x_i}$ with $\chi_S(x+y) = \chi_S(x)\chi_S(y)$ (Def. 1.2). With inner product $\langle f, g\rangle = 2^{-n}\sum_x f(x)g(x) = \mathbb{E}_{\mathbf x}[f(\mathbf x) g(\mathbf x)]$ (Def. 1.3), the $2^n$ parities form an orthonormal basis: $\langle \chi_S, \chi_T\rangle = \mathbf 1[S = T]$. Proof rests on $\chi_S \chi_T = \chi_{S \triangle T}$ (Fact 1.6) and $\mathbb{E}[\chi_S] = \mathbf 1[S = \emptyset]$ (Fact 1.7).
- **Basic Fourier formulas (section 1.4, p.25 to 27).** Coefficient formula $\widehat f(S) = \langle f, \chi_S\rangle = \mathbb{E}[f(\mathbf x)\chi_S(\mathbf x)]$ (Prop. 1.8). Parseval: $\langle f, f\rangle = \mathbb{E}[f^2] = \sum_S \widehat f(S)^2$, and for Boolean-valued $f$, $\sum_S \widehat f(S)^2 = 1$. Plancherel: $\langle f, g\rangle = \sum_S \widehat f(S)\widehat g(S)$. Mean $\mathbb{E}[f] = \widehat f(\emptyset)$ (Fact 1.12); variance $\mathbf{Var}[f] = \sum_{S \ne \emptyset}\widehat f(S)^2$ (Prop. 1.13), and for Boolean $f$, $\mathbf{Var}[f] = 4\Pr[f=1]\Pr[f=-1] \in [0,1]$ (Fact 1.14). $\langle f, g\rangle = 1 - 2\,\mathrm{dist}(f,g)$ for Boolean $f,g$ (Prop. 1.9), where $\mathrm{dist}(f,g) = \Pr_x[f(\mathbf x) \ne g(\mathbf x)]$ is relative Hamming distance.
- **Degree and Fourier weight (Defs. 1.17, 1.19, p.27 to 28).** Weight on $S$ is $\widehat f(S)^2$; weight at degree $k$ is $\mathbf{W}^k[f] = \sum_{|S|=k}\widehat f(S)^2 = \|f^{=k}\|_2^2$ where $f^{=k} = \sum_{|S|=k}\widehat f(S)\chi_S$. Real degree $\deg(f)$ (defined in notation and Exercise 1.10) is the max $|S|$ with $\widehat f(S) \ne 0$, equivalently the least $k$ such that $f$ is a real linear combination of $k$-juntas. The "spectral sample" $\mathcal{S}_f$ (Def. 1.18) draws $S$ with probability $\widehat f(S)^2$.
- **Densities and convolution (section 1.5, p.28 to 30).** Density $\varphi : \mathbb{F}_2^n \to \mathbb{R}^{\ge 0}$ with $\mathbb{E}[\varphi] = 1$. Convolution $(f * g)(x) = \mathbb{E}_{\mathbf y}[f(\mathbf y)g(x - \mathbf y)]$; the key theorem is $\widehat{f * g}(S) = \widehat f(S)\widehat g(S)$ (Theorem 1.27): convolution is multiplication of Fourier coefficients.
- **Highlight, the BLR linearity test (section 1.6, p.31 to 33).** $f : \mathbb{F}_2^n \to \mathbb{F}_2$ is linear iff $f(x+y) = f(x)+f(y)$ iff $f = \chi_S$ for some $S$. The BLR test queries $f$ at $\mathbf x, \mathbf y, \mathbf x + \mathbf y$ and accepts if $f(\mathbf x) + f(\mathbf y) = f(\mathbf x + \mathbf y)$. Theorem 1.30: if it accepts with probability $1 - \epsilon$ then $f$ is $\epsilon$-close to linear. The proof relates acceptance to $\tfrac12 + \tfrac12\sum_S \widehat f(S)^3$ (eq. 1.10) via Plancherel and the convolution theorem, then bounds $\sum_S \widehat f(S)^3 \le \max_S\widehat f(S)$. This is the prototype "combinatorial property read off the cubed spectrum" argument. Local correctability of linear functions (Prop. 1.31) is a 2-query corollary.

### Chapter 2: Basic concepts and social choice (read in full)

- **Pivotality and influence (Defs. 2.12, 2.13, p.46).** Coordinate $i$ is pivotal for $f$ at $x$ if $f(x) \ne f(x^{\oplus i})$. The influence $\mathbf{Inf}_i[f] = \Pr_{\mathbf x}[f(\mathbf x) \ne f(\mathbf x^{\oplus i})]$. Geometrically it is the fraction of dimension-$i$ edges that are boundary edges (Fact 2.14).
- **Discrete derivative and the Fourier formula for influence (Defs. 2.16, 2.17; Theorem 2.20, p.47 to 48).** $\mathrm{D}_i f(x) = \tfrac{f(x^{(i\to 1)}) - f(x^{(i\to -1)})}{2}$, which does not depend on $x_i$ and acts as formal differentiation: $\mathrm{D}_i f = \sum_{S \ni i}\widehat f(S)\, x^{S\setminus i}$ (Prop. 2.19). Then $\mathbf{Inf}_i[f] = \mathbb{E}[\mathrm{D}_i f(\mathbf x)^2] = \|\mathrm{D}_i f\|_2^2 = \sum_{S \ni i}\widehat f(S)^2$ (Theorem 2.20). For monotone $f$, $\mathbf{Inf}_i[f] = \widehat f(i)$ (Prop. 2.21, where $\widehat f(i)$ abbreviates $\widehat f(\{i\})$).
- **Expectation and Laplacian operators (Defs. 2.23, 2.25; Prop. 2.24, 2.26, p.48 to 49).** $\mathrm{E}_i f = \mathbb{E}_{\mathbf x_i}[f]$ isolates the part not depending on $x_i$; the decomposition $f = x_i \mathrm{D}_i f + \mathrm{E}_i f$ drives induction-on-$n$ proofs. Coordinate Laplacian $\mathrm{L}_i f = f - \mathrm{E}_i f = x_i \mathrm{D}_i f = \sum_{S \ni i}\widehat f(S) x^S$, with $\langle f, \mathrm{L}_i f\rangle = \mathbf{Inf}_i[f]$.
- **Total influence (Def. 2.27, section 2.3, p.49 to 52).** $\mathbf{I}[f] = \sum_{i=1}^n \mathbf{Inf}_i[f]$. For Boolean $f$ it equals average sensitivity $\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf x}[\mathrm{sens}_f(\mathbf x)]$ (Prop. 2.28), and the fraction of edges in the cube that are boundary edges is $\tfrac1n\mathbf{I}[f]$ (Fact 2.29). Fourier formula (Theorem 2.38): $\mathbf{I}[f] = \sum_S |S|\widehat f(S)^2 = \sum_k k\,\mathbf{W}^k[f] = \mathbb{E}_{S \sim \mathcal{S}_f}[|S|]$. Gradient $\nabla f = (\mathrm{D}_1 f,\dots,\mathrm{D}_n f)$ gives $\mathbf{I}[f] = \mathbb{E}[\|\nabla f\|_2^2]$ (Prop. 2.35). Examples (Example 2.30): constants have $\mathbf{I} = 0$, parity $\chi_{[n]}$ has $\mathbf{I} = n$, dictators have $\mathbf{I} = 1$, $\mathrm{OR}_n / \mathrm{AND}_n$ have $n 2^{1-n}$, and $\mathrm{Maj}_n$ has $\mathbf{I} \approx \sqrt{2/\pi}\sqrt{n}$.
- **Poincare Inequality and small-set expansion (p.52).** $\mathbf{Var}[f] \le \mathbf{I}[f]$ (immediate from the Fourier formulas), equality only for $\pm 1$ or $\pm\chi_i$. This is an edge-isoperimetric / edge-expansion bound for the cube. Theorem 2.39: for $\alpha = \min\{\Pr[f=1],\Pr[f=-1]\}$, $2\alpha\log(1/\alpha) \le \mathbf{I}[f]$. The recurring slogan: the Hamming cube is a "small-set expander."
- **Noise stability and sensitivity (section 2.4, Defs. 2.40 to 2.43, p.53 to 54).** $\mathbf y \sim N_\rho(x)$ keeps each bit with probability $\tfrac12 + \tfrac12\rho$ and flips it otherwise (for $\rho \in [-1,1]$); $(\mathbf x, \mathbf y)$ is a $\rho$-correlated pair. Noise stability $\mathbf{Stab}_\rho[f] = \mathbb{E}_{\rho\text{-corr}}[f(\mathbf x)f(\mathbf y)]$. Noise sensitivity $\mathbf{NS}_\delta[f] = \tfrac12 - \tfrac12\mathbf{Stab}_{1-2\delta}[f] = \Pr[f(\mathbf x)\ne f(\mathbf y)]$ when $\mathbf y$ flips each bit independently with probability $\delta$. Key fact: $\mathbf{Stab}_\rho[\chi_S] = \rho^{|S|}$ (Example 2.44).
- **Noise operator and the Fourier formula for stability (Def. 2.46, Prop. 2.47, Theorem 2.49, p.55 to 56).** $\mathrm{T}_\rho f(x) = \mathbb{E}_{\mathbf y \sim N_\rho(x)}[f(\mathbf y)]$, with $\mathrm{T}_\rho f = \sum_S \rho^{|S|}\widehat f(S)\chi_S = \sum_k \rho^k f^{=k}$. Then $\mathbf{Stab}_\rho[f] = \langle f, \mathrm{T}_\rho f\rangle = \sum_S \rho^{|S|}\widehat f(S)^2 = \sum_k \rho^k\mathbf{W}^k[f]$ (Theorem 2.49). So stability is a degree-attenuated (exponentially in degree) sum of Fourier weights. Dictators maximize noise stability among unbiased functions (Prop. 2.50). Derivative identities (Prop. 2.51): $\tfrac{d}{d\rho}\mathbf{Stab}_\rho[f]|_{\rho=0} = \mathbf{W}^1[f]$ and $|_{\rho=1} = \mathbf{I}[f]$.
- **$\rho$-stable influence (Def. 2.52, Prop. 2.54, p.57).** $\mathbf{Inf}_i^{(\rho)}[f] = \mathbf{Stab}_\rho[\mathrm{D}_i f] = \sum_{S \ni i}\rho^{|S|-1}\widehat f(S)^2$. Crucial structural fact: every $f$ has at most $O(1)$ "stably influential" coordinates: if $J = \{i : \mathbf{Inf}_i^{(1-\delta)}[f] \ge \epsilon\}$ then $|J| \le \tfrac{1}{\delta\epsilon}$ (Prop. 2.54). Ordinary influences do not have this bound (parity has all $n$ influences equal to 1). This bounded-notable-coordinates phenomenon is exactly the kind of structural quantization the project's "bounded-interface reconstruction" fourth barrier hypothesizes.
- **Arrow's Theorem and FKN (section 2.5, p.57 to 61).** Kalai's Fourier proof: $\Pr[\exists\text{ Condorcet winner}] = \tfrac34 - \tfrac34\mathbf{Stab}_{-1/3}[f]$ (Theorem 2.56), so a paradox-free rule must have $\mathbf{W}^1[f] = 1$, hence be a dictator. The robust version (Cor. 2.60): a $1-\epsilon$ paradox-free rule is $O(\epsilon)$-close to $\pm\chi_i$, via the **Friedgut-Kalai-Naor (FKN) Theorem** (stated p.61): if $f : \{-1,1\}^n \to \{-1,1\}$ has $\mathbf{W}^1[f] \ge 1-\delta$ then $f$ is $O(\delta)$-close to $\pm\chi_i$ for some $i$. The book defers the FKN proof to Chapter 9.1 (hypercontractivity) and notes the closeness can be sharpened to $\delta/4 + O(\delta^2\log(2/\delta))$ in Chapter 5.4.

### Chapter 3: Spectral structure and learning (sections 3.1 to 3.5 read in full)

- **Spectral norms, sparsity, granularity (Defs. 3.8 to 3.10, p.72).** $\|\widehat f\|_p = (\sum_\gamma |\widehat f(\gamma)|^p)^{1/p}$; sparsity$(\widehat f) = |\mathrm{supp}(\widehat f)|$; $\widehat f$ is $\epsilon$-granular if every coefficient is an integer multiple of $\epsilon$.
- **Subspaces and decision trees (section 3.2, p.72 to 74).** Indicators of affine subspaces / subcubes have explicit Fourier expansions (Props. 3.11, 3.12). Decision trees (Def. 3.13): a tree of size $s$ (leaves) and depth $k$ computing $f$ gives $\deg(f) \le k$, sparsity$(\widehat f) \le s 2^k \le 4^k$, $\|\widehat f\|_1 \le \|f\|_\infty s$, and $2^{-k}$-granularity (Prop. 3.16). A size-$s$ decision tree has its spectrum $\epsilon$-concentrated on degree up to $\log(s/\epsilon)$ (Prop. 3.17).
- **Restrictions (section 3.3, Def. 3.18, Props. 3.21, 3.22, p.74 to 78).** $f_{J|z}$ fixes the coordinates outside $J$ to $z$. The restricted Fourier coefficients satisfy $\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf z}[\widehat{f_{J|\mathbf z}}(S)] = \widehat f(S)$ and $\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf z}[\widehat{f_{J|\mathbf z}}(S)^2] = \sum_{T\subseteq \bar J}\widehat f(S\cup T)^2$ (Cor. 3.22). Poisson Summation Formula (p.78): $\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf h \sim H}[f(\mathbf h + z)] = \sum_{\gamma \in H^\perp}\chi_\gamma(z)\widehat f(\gamma)$.
- **Learning theory and the Low-Degree Algorithm (sections 3.4, 3.5, p.78 to 82).** PAC learning under uniform distribution (Def. 3.27). The reduction theorem (Theorem 3.29): if a learner can identify a collection $\mathcal F$ on which $f$'s spectrum is $\epsilon/2$-concentrated, it estimates those coefficients (Prop. 3.30, Chernoff) and outputs $h = \mathrm{sgn}(g)$ with $g = \sum_{S\in\mathcal F}\widetilde f(S)\chi_S$, achieving $\mathrm{dist}(f,h) \le \epsilon$ (Prop. 3.31). The **Low-Degree Algorithm** (p.81): if every $f$ in a class is $\epsilon/2$-concentrated up to degree $k$, take $\mathcal F = \{S : |S| \le k\}$ ($|\mathcal F| = O(n^k)$) and learn from random examples in time $\mathrm{poly}(n^k, 1/\epsilon)$. Consequences: classes with $\mathbf{I}[f] \le t$ learn in $n^{O(t/\epsilon)}$ (Cor. 3.32); monotone functions in $n^{O(\sqrt n/\epsilon)}$ (Cor. 3.33); $\mathbf{NS}_\delta[f] \le \epsilon/6$ classes in $\mathrm{poly}(n^{1/\delta},1/\epsilon)$ (Cor. 3.34). Goldreich-Levin (section 3.5) is the query-access analogue. This chapter is the precise statement of the "low-degree concentration implies learnability" engine that meta-complexity and MCSP arguments lean on.

### Chapter 4: DNF formulas and small-depth circuits (sections 4.2 to 4.5 read in full)

- **Tribes and the KKL Theorem (section 4.2, p.96 to 97).** $\mathrm{Tribes}_{w,s}$ is a width-$w$, $s$-term read-once DNF. With the balancing choice ($w \approx \log n - \log\log n$, $s \approx n/\log n$) it is essentially unbiased yet every influence is $\mathbf{Inf}_i = \tfrac{\ln n}{n}(1 \pm o(1))$ (Props. 4.12, 4.13), so $\mathbf{I} \approx \ln n$. **Kahn-Kalai-Linial (KKL) Theorem** (p.97): for any $f : \{-1,1\}^n \to \{-1,1\}$, $\mathbf{MaxInf}[f] \ge \mathbf{Var}[f]\cdot\Omega(\tfrac{\log n}{n})$. Tribes is tight up to constants. Proved in Chapter 9 (hypercontractivity).
- **Random restrictions (section 4.3, Def. 4.15, Prop. 4.17, p.98 to 99).** A $\delta$-random restriction $(\mathbf J\mid\mathbf z)$ keeps each coordinate free with probability $\delta$. $\mathbb{E}[\widehat{f_{\mathbf J|\mathbf z}}(S)] = \delta^{|S|}\widehat f(S)$ and $\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{Inf}_i[f_{\mathbf J|\mathbf z}]] = \delta\,\mathbf{Inf}_i[f]$, hence $\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{I}[f_{\mathbf J|\mathbf z}]] = \delta\,\mathbf{I}[f]$ (Cor. 4.18). Consequence: size-$s$ DNFs have $\mathbf{I}[f] \le O(\log s)$ (Theorem 4.20).
- **Hastad's Switching Lemma (section 4.4, p.100 to 102).** Stated (proof referred to Hastad / Razborov-Beame): for a width-$w$ DNF or CNF under a $\delta$-random restriction, $\Pr[\mathrm{DT}(f_{\mathbf J|\mathbf z}) \ge k] \le (5\delta w)^k$, with no dependence on size or $n$. Corollaries pinned in the book: width-$w$ DNFs have spectra $\epsilon$-concentrated up to degree $O(w\log(1/\epsilon))$ (Theorem 4.22); low-degree Fourier 1-norm bound $\sum_{|U|\le k}|\widehat f(U)| \le 2(20w)^k$ (Theorem 4.24); and Mansour's-Conjecture-adjacent concentration on collections of size $w^{O(w\log(1/\epsilon))}$ (Theorem 4.25).
- **LMN and $\mathsf{AC}^0$ (section 4.5, p.103 to 106).** Lemma 4.28 shows random restrictions compose and reduce depth-$d$ circuits layer by layer. **LMN Theorem** (Linial-Mansour-Nisan, p.106): a depth-$d$ size-$s$ circuit has its spectrum $\epsilon$-concentrated up to degree $O(\log(s/\epsilon))^{d-1}\cdot\log(1/\epsilon)$. Consequence (Theorem 4.31): $\mathsf{AC}^0$ (poly-size constant-depth) is learnable in quasipolynomial time $n^{O(\log n)^d}$. Also $\mathbf{I}[f] \le O(\log s)^{d-1}$ for depth-$d$ size-$s$ circuits (Theorem 4.30). The original motivation, parity $\chi_{[n]}$ is not in $\mathsf{AC}^0$, follows because parity has all weight at degree $n$.

### Chapter 5: Majority and threshold functions (sections 5.1 to 5.5 read in full) (most project-relevant)

- **LTFs, PTFs, sparsity (section 5.1, p.116 to 118).** A polynomial threshold function $f = \mathrm{sgn}(p(x))$ has sparsity $\le s$ if $p$ has $\le s$ monomials (Def. 5.7); this is the size of the threshold-of-parities circuit. Chow / degree-$k$ extension (Theorem 5.8): a degree-$k$ PTF is determined by its low-degree Fourier coefficients. Theorem 5.9: a degree-$k$ PTF has $\mathbf{W}^{\le k}[f] \ge e^{-2k}$ (proof uses the hypercontractive $\|p\|_2 \le e^k\|p\|_1$, deferred to Theorem 9.22). Theorem 5.10: a PTF over monomial collection $\mathcal F$ has $\sum_{S\in\mathcal F}|\widehat f(S)| \ge 1$. Corollary 5.11: the inner-product-mod-2 function $\mathrm{IP}_{2n}$ requires PTF sparsity $\ge 2^n$ (every $|\widehat{\mathrm{IP}}_{2n}(S)| = 2^{-n}$). Theorem 5.12 / Cor. 5.13: any $f$ with small Fourier 1-norm has a sparse approximating PTF; $f$ is expressible as a majority of $s = \lceil 4n\|\widehat f\|_1^2\rceil$ parities or negated-parities (probabilistic method, Chernoff). These are exactly threshold-circuit ($\mathsf{TC}^0$-adjacent) lower-bound and upper-bound statements.
- **Majority and the CLT (section 5.2, p.118 to 121).** $\mathrm{Maj}_n(x) = \mathrm{sgn}(\sum_i \tfrac{1}{\sqrt n}x_i)$. CLT gives $\mathbf{I}[\mathrm{Maj}_n] \sim \sqrt{2/\pi}\sqrt n$. Berry-Esseen (quantitative CLT, p.119) bounds the error. For any unbiased LTF $\mathrm{sgn}(\sum a_i x_i)$ with all $|a_i| \le \epsilon$, $|\mathbf{Stab}_\rho[f] - \tfrac{2}{\pi}\arcsin\rho| \le O(\epsilon/\sqrt{1-\rho^2})$ (Theorem 5.17). The limiting majority noise stability is $\lim_n \mathbf{Stab}_\rho[\mathrm{Maj}_n] = \tfrac{2}{\pi}\arcsin\rho$ (Theorem 2.45 / 5.18), via Sheppard's Formula and the 2D CLT. The Majority Is Stablest Theorem is stated (p.121, full proof deferred to Chapter 11): among unbiased small-influence functions, Majority has the largest noise stability.
- **Fourier coefficients of Majority (section 5.3, p.121 to 124).** $\widehat{\mathrm{Maj}}_n(S) = 0$ for $|S|$ even (odd function); for $|S| = k$ odd, the explicit formula is Theorem 5.19. Asymptotics (Theorem 5.22, eq. 5.10, 5.11): for fixed odd $k$, $\mathbf{W}^k[\mathrm{Maj}_n] \to [\rho^k](\tfrac{2}{\pi}\arcsin\rho)$, with $\mathbf{W}^k(\mathrm{Maj}) \sim (\tfrac{2}{\pi})^{3/2}k^{-3/2}$ and tail $\mathbf{W}^{>k}(\mathrm{Maj}) \sim (\tfrac{2}{\pi})^{3/2}k^{-1/2}$. Hence the spectrum is $\epsilon$-concentrated on degree up to $\tfrac{8}{\pi^3}\epsilon^{-2} + O_\epsilon(1)$ (Cor. 5.23). $\mathbf{W}^1(\mathrm{Maj}) = \tfrac{2}{\pi}$ is the value to remember. NOTE for the project: this $k^{-1/2}$ tail (weight is spread to high degree, not concentrated) is the analytic shadow of the fact MAJORITY needs polynomial degree about $\sqrt n$ to be approximated. The book does not state approximate degree as such, but this Fourier-tail asymptotic is the same phenomenon viewed through $L^2$ weight.
- **Degree-1 weight (section 5.4, p.124 to 130).** $\mathbf{W}^1[f] = \sum_i \widehat f(i)^2$; $\mathbf{W}^1[f] = \tfrac{d}{d\rho}\mathbf{Stab}_\rho[f]|_{\rho=0}$. **Level-1 Inequality** (p.126): $f : \{-1,1\}^n \to \{0,1\}$ with mean $\alpha \le 1/2$ has $\mathbf{W}^1[f] \le O(\alpha^2\log(1/\alpha))$ (sharp form $2\alpha^2\ln(1/\alpha)$, Remark 5.28; called Chang's Inequality in additive combinatorics). The **$\tfrac{2}{\pi}$ Theorem** (p.127): if all $|\widehat f(i)| \le \epsilon$ then $\mathbf{W}^1[f] \le \tfrac{2}{\pi} + O(\epsilon)$, and if $\mathbf{W}^1[f] \ge \tfrac{2}{\pi} - \epsilon$ then $f$ is $O(\sqrt\epsilon)$-close to the LTF $\mathrm{sgn}(f^{=1})$. Proofs use Hoeffding / CLT applied to the random sum $L(x) = \sum_i \widehat f(i)x_i$ and the Gaussian isoperimetric function $\mathcal U = \phi\circ\Phi^{-1}$. **Sharp FKN** (Theorem 5.33, p.129): given any FKN bound with constant $C$, the closeness can be bootstrapped to $\delta/4 + 16C^2\delta^2\max(\log(1/C\delta),1)$, via restriction and the Level-1 / Cor. 5.32 inequality.
- **Peres's Theorem and uniform noise stability (section 5.5, p.130 to 132).** **Peres's Theorem**: every LTF $f$ has $\mathbf{NS}_\delta[f] \le O(\sqrt\delta)$, with a simple proof (no CLT) via Theorem 5.35, a general "average-sensitivity bounds noise sensitivity" technique for classes closed under negation and variable identification. Consequence: the class of LTFs is uniformly noise-stable, hence has spectrum $\epsilon$-concentrated up to degree $O(1/\epsilon^2)$ (Prop. 3.3), and an AND of $s$ LTFs is learnable in $n^{O(s^2/\epsilon^2)}$ (the only known way to learn even an AND of two LTFs to constant error in $\mathrm{poly}(n)$). The Benjamini-Kalai-Schramm conjecture ($\sqrt{2/\pi}$ optimal constant) is noted as open.

## Techniques and proof ideas

A builder reusing this book will draw on a small number of recurring moves.

1. **Read combinatorics off the spectrum.** Mean is $\widehat f(\emptyset)$, variance is the nonempty weight, influence of $i$ is the weight on sets containing $i$, total influence is the weight weighted by degree, noise stability is the weight attenuated by $\rho^{|S|}$. Every quantity of interest becomes a linear or near-linear functional of $\{\widehat f(S)^2\}$. The pattern "express the acceptance probability or correlation, expand via Parseval / Plancherel, then bound a weighted sum of squared coefficients" is the engine (BLR Theorem 1.30, Arrow Theorem 2.56, the degree-1 theorems).
2. **The noise operator $\mathrm{T}_\rho$ as a smoothing / degree-attenuation tool.** $\mathrm{T}_\rho$ multiplies degree-$k$ weight by $\rho^k$; differentiating $\mathbf{Stab}_\rho$ at $\rho = 0$ and $\rho = 1$ extracts $\mathbf{W}^1$ and $\mathbf{I}$ respectively. Stable influences quantize the notable coordinates to $O(1)$ many (Prop. 2.54).
3. **Restrictions and the random-restriction method.** Fixing coordinates simplifies a function; a $\delta$-random restriction scales each coefficient by $\delta^{|S|}$ in expectation and each influence by $\delta$. Composing restrictions reduces circuit depth. This is the whole Switching-Lemma / LMN machine: random-restrict a small-depth circuit until it collapses to a shallow decision tree, then use the clean decision-tree spectrum (low degree, granular, small 1-norm).
4. **CLT / Berry-Esseen and the Gaussian limit.** For LTFs $\mathrm{sgn}(\sum a_i x_i)$ with no dominant coefficient, the random sum behaves like a Gaussian, so noise stability tends to $\tfrac{2}{\pi}\arcsin\rho$ and degree-1 weight is controlled by Gaussian isoperimetry. This is the bridge to Chapter 11 Gaussian-space methods (the Invariance Principle is the rigorous version).
5. **The probabilistic method for sparse PTFs.** Sample monomials proportional to $|\widehat f(T)|/\|\widehat f\|_1$, Chernoff-concentrate, union-bound over inputs (Theorem 5.12). Conversely the $\sum_{S\in\mathcal F}|\widehat f(S)| \ge 1$ inequality (Theorem 5.10) lower-bounds PTF sparsity, giving the $2^n$ bound for inner-product-mod-2 (Cor. 5.11).
6. **Hypercontractivity as the heavy hammer (deferred).** The $\|p\|_2 \le e^k\|p\|_1$ type inequalities for low-degree polynomials power FKN, KKL, the degree-$k$ PTF weight bound, and Majority Is Stablest. These are in Chapters 9 to 11, which I did not read in full.
