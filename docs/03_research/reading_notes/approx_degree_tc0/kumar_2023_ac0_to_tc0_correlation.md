## Summary

The paper introduces a graded family of circuit classes $\mathsf{GC}^0(k)$ that interpolates continuously between $\mathsf{AC}^0$ (the $k=O(1)$ end) and $\mathsf{TC}^0$ (the $k = \Theta(n)$ end), where the gates are arbitrary unbounded fan-in functions that are required to be constant only on inputs of Hamming weight $\geq k$ (Definition 1.2, p. 4). Crucially this class captures "unbalanced" or biased linear threshold functions (Definition 1.3; the containment $\mathsf{TC}^0(k) \subset \mathsf{GC}^0(k)$ is Theorem A.1, p. 35). The core technical contribution is an optimal multi-switching lemma for $\mathsf{GC}^0(k)$ whose failure-probability bound differs from the $\mathsf{AC}^0$ bound only by replacing $m$ with $2^k m$ (Theorem 4.8, p. 19). This yields a "lifting" phenomenon: essentially any $\mathsf{AC}^0$ result provable via the switching lemma extends to $\mathsf{GC}^0(\log s)$ with no loss in parameters (Theorem 4.1, p. 15, and Corollary 4.11, p. 21). The author demonstrates this with four applications: parity correlation bounds, correlation against circuits with few threshold gates, PRGs, and Fourier-spectrum bounds.

## Key definitions and results

**The graded gate family (the central new object).**

- **Definition 1.1 ($\mathsf{AC}^0(k)$ circuits, p. 3).** A $k$-OR outputs 1 iff $\geq k$ input bits are 1; a $k$-AND outputs 0 iff $\geq k$ input bits are 0. $\mathsf{AC}^0(k)$ is the constant-depth class built from negations and $\{k'\text{-AND}, k'\text{-OR}\}$ for $k' \leq k$. Observation (p. 3): $\mathsf{AC}^0(1) = \mathsf{AC}^0$ and $\mathsf{AC}^0(s/2)$ at size $s$ captures $\mathsf{TC}^0$, so $k$ is the dial between the two. A size-$s$ $\mathsf{AC}^0_d$ lower bound transfers only as a weaker $s^{1/k}$ lower bound for $\mathsf{AC}^0_{d-1}(k)$ (p. 4), so the relationship is already nontrivial for $k = \omega(1)$.

- **Definition 1.2 / 3.2 ($\mathsf{G}(k)$ gates / $\mathsf{GC}^0(k)$, pp. 4, 12).** $\mathsf{G}(k)$ is the set of all arbitrary unbounded fan-in gates that are constant on all inputs with $\geq k$ ones (called "orlike") or constant on all inputs with $\geq k$ zeros (called "andlike"). $\mathsf{GC}^0(k)$ is the constant-depth class over $\mathsf{G}(k)$ gates. This generalizes $\mathsf{AC}^0(k)$ and is the class all results are actually proved for.

- **Definition 1.3 / 3.3 (balanced LTFs / $\mathsf{TC}^0(k)$, pp. 4, 13).** For an LTF $f(x) = \mathrm{sgn}(\sum w_i x_i - \theta)$ with weights sorted by magnitude $|w_{\sigma(1)}| \leq \cdots \leq |w_{\sigma(n)}|$, the balance $\mathrm{bal}(f)$ is the smallest $j$ with $-\sum_{i \leq j} |w_i| + \sum_{i > j} |w_i| < |\theta|$. $\mathsf{TC}^0(k)$ is the constant-depth class of THR gates with balance $\leq k$. Intuition: balance measures how far the threshold $\theta$ is shifted away from the regime where the small-magnitude weights can flip the sign. A biased MAJORITY (threshold pushed to weight $n^{1/d}$) has small balance.

- **Theorem A.1 (p. 35).** Any THR gate with balance $\leq k$ is, up to negating certain input bits and the output, in $\mathsf{G}(k)$. This is the bridge that makes $\mathsf{GC}^0(k)$ a genuine model of "$\mathsf{AC}^0$ with the power of majority over $\geq k$ bits," and it is why the whole apparatus is relevant to threshold circuits.

**The core lemma and the lifting principle.**

- **Theorem 4.1 / 1.4 (multi-switching / simplification, pp. 15, 5).** The proper statement (Theorem 4.1, p. 15): for $F$ a depth-2 $\mathsf{G}(k) \circ \{\text{AND}, \text{OR}\}_w$ circuit, $\Lambda$ a $(t+w)$-wise $p$-bounded subset, and $x$ uniform, $\Pr[\mathsf{DT}(F|_{\rho(\Lambda, x)}) \geq t] \leq (20pw)^t 2^k$. The multi-switching version (Theorem 4.8, p. 19): for a list $\mathcal{F} = \{F_1, \ldots, F_m\}$ of $\mathsf{G}(k) \circ \text{AND}_w$ circuits, $\Pr_{\rho \sim R_p}[\mathcal{F}|_\rho \text{ has no } r\text{-partial depth-}t\ \mathsf{DT}] \leq 4(64(2^k m)^{1/r} pw)^t$.

- **The lifting principle (Remark after Theorem 4.8, p. 19).** The only difference from the standard $\mathsf{AC}^0$ multi-switching bound (Lyu 2022) is that every occurrence of $m$ is multiplied by $2^k$. So if the constant in the exponent can be ignored, the $\mathsf{GC}^0(k)$ bound matches the $\mathsf{AC}^0$ bound whenever $2^k \approx m$, i.e. $k \approx \log m \approx \log s$. This is the engine of every application: size-$s$ $\mathsf{AC}^0$ results lift to $\mathsf{GC}^0(\log s)$ "with no loss in parameters." Stated as a slogan in the abstract: depth-$d$ size-$s$ $\mathsf{AC}^0$ results lift to depth-$d$ size-$s^{.99}$ $\mathsf{GC}^0(.01 \log s)$.

- **Corollary 4.11 (p. 21).** For $C$ a size-$m$ $\mathsf{GC}^0_d(k)$ circuit and $p = 1/(40(128(k+\log m))^{d-1})$, $\Pr_{\rho \sim R_p}[\mathsf{DT}(C|_\rho) \geq t] \leq 2 \cdot 2^{-t/2^{d-1} + k}$. This is the depth-$d$ simplification statement that drives the correlation and Fourier results.

**The four applications.**

- **Theorem 1.5 / 5.1 + Corollary 5.2 (optimal parity correlation, pp. 5, 22).** A size-$m$ depth-$d$ $\mathsf{GC}^0(k)$ circuit $C$ has $|\mathbb{E}_x[(-1)^{C(x) + \mathsf{PAR}(x)}]| \leq 2^{-\Omega_d(n/(k + \log m)^{d-1}) + k}$. Consequently (Corollary 5.2, p. 22), for $0 \leq k \leq .1 n^{1/d}$, computing $\mathsf{PAR}_n$ requires $\mathsf{GC}^0_d(k)$ size $2^{\Omega((n/k)^{1/(d-1)})}$. The headline reading (p. 5): a $2^{\Omega(n^{1/d})}$ size lower bound for $\mathsf{GC}^0_d(\Omega(n^{1/d}))$ circuits, essentially matching the classical $2^{\Omega(n^{1/(d-1)})}$ $\mathsf{AC}^0$ lower bound, even though $\mathsf{GC}^0_d(\Omega(n^{1/d}))$ contains gates not computable by $2^{\Omega(n^{1/2d})}$-size $\mathsf{AC}^0_d$ circuits. So shifting a majority threshold to $n^{1/d}$ adds real power yet has no effect on the ability to compute parity.

- **The sharp-threshold sensitivity (p. 5).** This is the load-bearing structural fact for the strand. The bound is tight up to the hidden constant in the $\Omega(\cdot)$: $\mathsf{PAR}_{n^{1/d}} \in \mathsf{G}(n^{1/d})$, and a depth-$d$ tree of $\mathsf{PAR}_{n^{1/d}}$ gates (layer $i$ from the bottom has $n^{1-i/d}$ gates) computes $\mathsf{PAR}_n$ with a depth-$d$ size-$O(n^{1-1/d})$ circuit, which is sublinear. So at $k = n^{1/d}$ the exponential lower bound collapses to a sublinear upper bound: cross the constant and you fall off a cliff. Theorem A.3 (p. 37) and Theorem A.4 (p. 38) make this tightness rigorous for both size and correlation.

- **Theorems 1.6 / 5.3 / 5.4 (few arbitrary threshold gates, pp. 6, 23).** Starting from a base $\mathsf{GC}^0(\log^2 n)$ circuit augmented with $g$ arbitrary THR gates and uncorrelating against the Razborov-Widgerson function $\mathsf{RW} \in \mathsf{P}$ (Theorem 5.4, p. 23): $\mathbb{E}[(-1)^{C(x) + \mathsf{RW}(x)}] \leq 2^{-\Omega(n^{.499}/g - g)}$, so with $g = \Theta(n^{.249})$ THR gates the correlation is $\leq 2^{-\Omega(n^{.249})}$ (Theorem 1.6). An analogous $2^{-\Omega(n^{.499})}$ bound holds for $n^{.499}$ arbitrary symmetric gates. This extends Servedio-Tan 2018 by starting from a richer base. The flagged obstruction (Remark, p. 24): naively expanding the $\mathsf{GC}^0(\log^2 n)$ base into an $\mathsf{AC}^0$ circuit makes the proof fail, because after pruning fan-in one is left uncorrelating against arbitrary $\omega(\log n)$-party NOF protocols, a longstanding open problem (Problem 6.21 in Kushilevitz-Nisan).

- **Theorems 1.7 / 5.7 / 5.8 (PRGs, pp. 6, 25-27).** There is an $\varepsilon$-PRG for size-$m$ $\mathsf{GC}^0_d(\log m)$ with seed length $O(\log^{d-1} m \log(m/\varepsilon) \log\log m)$, matching the best $\mathsf{AC}^0$ PRG (Lyu 2022) up to the $\log\log m$ factor. Built by fully derandomizing Theorem 4.8 (the derandomized multi-switching lemma is Theorem 5.5, p. 24, with full proof deferred to Theorem A.11) and using the partition-based template. Naively expanding to a width-$\log m$ CNF/DNF would give a size $\approx m^{\log m}$ $\mathsf{AC}^0_{d+1}$ circuit and a suboptimal seed.

- **Theorems 1.8 / 5.13 / 5.14 (Fourier spectrum, pp. 7, 28-29).** For a size-$m$ $\mathsf{GC}^0_d(k)$ circuit with $t := (k + \log m)^{d-1}$: exponentially small Fourier tails (ESFT) $W^{\geq \ell}[f] \leq C \cdot 2^k \cdot 2^{-\Omega(\ell/t)}$ (Theorem 5.14, item 1, p. 29). Via the Linial-Mansour-Nisan / Tal unification (Theorem 5.9, p. 27), ESFT bootstraps to bounded degree-$\ell$ influence, bounded level-$\ell$ $L_1$ norm, and Fourier mass concentration on $2^{O((k+\log(1/\varepsilon))t\log t)}$ coefficients. This yields a learning algorithm for $\mathsf{GC}^0_d(k)$ (Theorem 5.16, p. 30) and a new correlation bound: a random symmetric function uncorrelates with $\mathsf{GC}^0_d(k)$ circuits at rate $|\hat{g}(\emptyset)| + C_d k(k+\log m)^{d-1}/\sqrt{n}$ (Theorem 5.17, p. 31).

## Techniques and proof ideas

The whole architecture is the random-restriction / switching-lemma framework: show that under a random restriction the circuit simplifies (collapses to a small decision tree) while the hard target (parity, RW, a random symmetric function) survives, then average.

- **Lyu's witness/transcript proof of the switching lemma, adapted (Sections 2.1, 4).** The proof builds a canonical decision tree (CDT, Algorithm 1, p. 15) that scans the bottom-layer clauses left to right. The crucial new ingredient versus Lyu 2022 is a counter $ctr$ that tracks how many satisfied clauses have been seen before reaching the limit $k$ (the orlike threshold). The "witness" is the transcript of the CDT (Definition 4.3, p. 16). The key insight that makes the union bound feasible is that a partial witness has a unique completion (Claim 4.6, p. 17), and a witness searcher $\mathcal{S}$ (Algorithm 2, p. 18) can reconstruct the full witness from a partial one with probability $2^{-s}$ over an advice string (Lemma 4.7, p. 18). The whole reason $2^k$ appears (and only $2^k$) is the count of partial witnesses: the choices of $(r, s_i)$ multiply out to $\leq 2^{2s+k}$ (p. 19), where the extra $2^k$ is "a side effect of looking at a more complicated circuit class." This is the precise mechanism by which $k$ enters the bound additively in the exponent.

- **The depth-reduction lemma via unambiguous DNFs (Section 2.2 p. 10, Lemma 4.9 p. 20).** The naive depth reduction fails for $k$-OR: a $k$-OR $\circ$ OR is not itself a $k$-OR. The fix is the observation that the DNF unraveled from a decision tree is unambiguous (any assignment satisfies at most one AND clause), so the number of satisfied middle-layer OR clauses equals the number of satisfied bottom AND clauses, and the $k$-OR over ORs collapses to a $k$-OR over the AND clauses. This is the structural property that lets the switching lemma be iterated through constant depth (Theorem 4.10, p. 21). A builder reusing this should note that the argument genuinely needs decision-tree structure, not just small width.

- **The lifting move a builder would reuse.** The central reusable trick (Remark, p. 19): rather than expanding a function to a width-$w$ CNF and applying the classical switching lemma, express it as a more compact $\mathsf{GC}^0(k) \circ \text{AND}_w$ circuit and apply Theorem 4.1 directly. The appendix Remark after Theorem A.2 (p. 37) shows this can give asymptotically stronger parameters: $\mathsf{PAR}_n$ as a $\mathsf{G}(t\log(n/t)) \circ \text{AND}_{n/t\log(n/t)}$ circuit yields a bound $\leq (20pm^2/t^2)^t$, stronger than the classical $(5pn)^t$ when $t = \omega(\sqrt n)$.

- **Tightness constructions (Appendix A.2).** Theorem A.2 (p. 36) shows $\mathsf{PAR}_{kw}$ saturates the switching-lemma bound $2^{k/2}(.5pw)^t$ via the unambiguity of parity CNFs. Theorem A.3 (p. 37) and Theorem A.4 (p. 38) give the matching upper-bound circuits for size and correlation, establishing the sharp threshold at $k = n^{1/d}$.

## Relevance to this project

This source is a direct, fine-grained map of the $\mathsf{TC}^0$ hinge identified in the 2050 backward-induction dossier: the place where the polynomial method dies because MAJORITY has approximate degree $\Theta(n)$ (Paturi 1992). The paper does not break the MAJORITY wall. Instead it charts exactly how far the random-restriction toolkit reaches up the gradient toward it, and it pins the precise gate-theoretic coordinate of the wall.

- **It quantifies the gradient.** The dial $k$ in $\mathsf{GC}^0(k)$ is exactly "how much majority power do we add to $\mathsf{AC}^0$." For $k$ up to $\sim n^{1/d}$ (biased majorities, biased LTFs), the random-restriction method still proves exponential lower bounds and small Fourier tails. The wall is not at $\mathsf{TC}^0$ in general; it is at the balance/bias parameter. A balanced majority ($\theta \approx 0$, balance $\approx n/2$) defeats the method; a majority whose threshold is shifted to $n^{1/d}$ does not. This refines the dossier's "MAJORITY wall" into a parameterized wall indexed by balance.

- **It is a barrier-discipline data point.** All techniques here (random restrictions, switching lemmas, Fourier analysis) are classic natural-proof-style and relativizing/algebrizing methods. They establish lower bounds against an explicit hard function (parity, RW) only for circuit classes weaker than full $\mathsf{TC}^0$. The fact that the method tops out at balanced LTFs is consistent with the project's finding that breaking $\mathsf{TC}^0$ needs a non-natural, non-algebrizing ingredient. The paper itself flags (Remark p. 24) that pushing past its base class runs into the $\omega(\log n)$-party NOF communication wall, an independent hard barrier.

- **It localizes the polynomial-method death.** The dossier hinge says MAJORITY's approximate degree $\Theta(\sqrt n)$ kills the polynomial method. Here the Fourier-tail bound (Theorem 5.14) is the dual statement: $\mathsf{GC}^0(k)$ has $W^{\geq \ell}[f] \leq C \cdot 2^k \cdot 2^{-\Omega(\ell/(k+\log m)^{d-1})}$, exponentially small tails. A balanced MAJORITY has Fourier weight at level $\sim \sqrt n$ that is not exponentially small. The paper's Theorem 5.17 discussion (p. 31) makes this explicit: the Fourier approach is "doomed to fail" for $\mathsf{AC}^0[\oplus]$ because parity has all its Fourier weight at level $n$. So the $\mathsf{GC}^0(k)$ ESFT is precisely a map of where the Fourier/approximate-degree method has room to run before the wall.

## What this enables / what remains open

Mapping to the four research directions:

- **Direction 01 (circuit lower bounds, Williams ACC0 toward P/poly).** This source enriches the layer just below $\mathsf{TC}^0$ with a continuum of intermediate classes and shows that the classical $\mathsf{AC}^0$ machinery reaches up to biased LTFs. It complements the Williams spine (which attacks $\mathsf{ACC}^0$ via the algorithm-to-lower-bound connection) by charting the threshold-circuit gradient that the Williams program eventually has to climb. The bootstrapping observation it cites (Chen-Tell 2019, p. 3): a slight improvement in the $\mathsf{TC}^0$ exponent constant would separate $\mathsf{TC}^0$ from $\mathsf{NC}^1$. Kumar's parity bounds are at depth $d$ rather than the $C_{AK} d^2$ depth that bootstrapping needs, so they do not trigger that collapse, but they mark the right coordinate.

- **Direction 02 (natural-proofs evasion, the MCSP crux).** The methods here are natural in the Razborov-Rudich sense (random restrictions and Fourier concentration give a large, constructive distinguisher). This is a clean illustration of LEARNINGS finding (2): natural proofs are common. The fact that the method caps out at balanced LTFs without ever needing a non-natural ingredient is consistent with finding (3): the constructivity crux is what blocks the final step.

- **Direction 03 (proof complexity, NP vs coNP).** Indirect. The unambiguous-DNF depth-reduction technique (Lemma 4.9) is the kind of structural collapse argument proof-complexity lower bounds reuse, but the paper does not address $\mathsf{NP}$ vs $\mathsf{coNP}$ directly.

- **Direction 04 (GCT, multiplicity obstructions).** No direct connection; the methods are combinatorial, not representation-theoretic.

LEARNINGS findings touched: (2) natural proofs are common (the method is natural and tops out without a non-natural ingredient); (5) average-case is not worst-case (everything here is correlation/average-case hardness, which by the dossier does not directly imply the worst-case separation); (6) Williams is the existence proof barriers are passable (Kumar's classical methods, by contrast, do not pass the $\mathsf{TC}^0$ barrier, sharpening where Williams-style non-naturality is required).

Concrete open questions and cruxes this source exposes (several stated explicitly in Section 6, p. 32):

1. **Is Theorem 4.1 tight for $\mathsf{AC}^0(k)$ or $\mathsf{TC}^0(k)$, not just $\mathsf{GC}^0(k)$?** The tightness constructions (Theorem A.2) abuse the full generality of $\mathsf{G}(k)$. If the pathological constructions can be confined to the weaker classes, the bounds might be improvable there. This is exactly the kind of "where does the wall actually sit" question the hinge strand needs answered.

2. **Can stronger lower bounds be obtained for $\mathsf{AC}^0(k)$ / $\mathsf{TC}^0(k)$ specifically?** Either a pathological construction inside these classes or a stronger lower bound would refine the wall's location.

3. **The Allender-Koucky reduction angle (p. 32).** $\mathsf{MAJ}_n$ is an $\mathsf{AC}^0(n^\varepsilon)$ circuit of depth $\leq C_{AK}/\varepsilon$ and size $O(n^{1+\varepsilon})$, built from only AND2, OR2, and $\mathsf{MAJ}_{n^\varepsilon}$. If all $\mathsf{G}(k)$ gates were allowed, could one find a lower-depth-blowup construction? That would strengthen the reduction from proving $\mathsf{TC}^0$ bounds to proving bounds on smaller $\mathsf{AC}^0(n^\varepsilon)$ circuits, i.e. it would lower the bar at the hinge.

4. **The depth gap at the hinge.** Beating the current $\mathsf{TC}^0$ depth-$d$ size-$\Omega(n^{1 + C_{IPS}^{-d}})$ lower bound reduces (per the paper, p. 6) to beating depth $C_{AK} d/\varepsilon$ size $n^{(1 + 50^{-d})(2+\varepsilon)}$ bounds for $\mathsf{GC}^0(n^\varepsilon)$. Kumar gets exponential bounds against parity at $\varepsilon = 1/d$ but at depth $d$, not $C_{AK} d^2$. Closing this depth gap (with a function other than parity, since parity falls off the cliff at $k = n^{1/d}$) is the open crux that would connect this gradient map to a genuine $\mathsf{TC}^0$ separation.

5. **Other applications of the generalized switching lemma.** The lemma can be substituted wherever the classical switching lemma is used; the appendix remark (p. 37) already shows it can give strictly stronger parameters for parity when $t = \omega(\sqrt n)$. This invites a systematic sweep of whether known $\mathsf{AC}^0$ results sharpen when re-derived through the compact-$\mathsf{GC}^0(k)$ representation.

The honest caveat for the dossier: this is a precise, well-charted map of the terrain immediately below the MAJORITY wall, not a route over it. It tells the BUILDER exactly which gate parameter ($k \approx$ balance $\approx n^{1/d}$) is the last one the natural random-restriction toolkit can handle, and it confirms that the wall is parameterized by LTF balance rather than by raw membership in $\mathsf{TC}^0$.
