## Summary
This is the standard reference survey on how algebraic topology is applied to genuinely combinatorial problems. Part I assembles worked examples where topology resolves a combinatorial question: evasiveness of monotone graph properties (Kahn-Saks-Sturtevant via a fixed-point argument on a $\mathbb{Z}/p$-acyclic complex), Kneser's conjecture (Lovasz via the Borsuk-Ulam $\mathbb{Z}/2$-index of the neighborhood complex), ham-sandwich and Tverberg-type partition theorems, oriented-matroid sign functions, and Hard Lefschetz applied to f-vectors of polytopes. Part II is a reference toolkit: simplicial complexes and order complexes of posets, homotopy and homology with the Euler-Poincare and Hopf trace formulas, the nerve lemma and fiber/quotient homotopy theorems, the structural hierarchy (vertex-decomposable implies shellable implies constructible implies Cohen-Macaulay), regular cell complexes, and the fixed-point / antipodality theorems (Brouwer, Lefschetz, Borsuk-Ulam). The contribution this note extracts for the project: a precise inventory of which invariants of a combinatorial complex are computable, which are homotopy invariants, and where $\mathbb{Z}$-coefficient torsion can and cannot enter.

## Key definitions and results

### Combinatorial complexes and the poset/complex bridge (Part II, sections 9 and 12)
- **Abstract simplicial complex (9.1, page 1842).** A pair $\Delta = (V, \Delta)$ with vertex set $V$ and a family of nonempty finite faces closed under nonempty subset. The empty set is a face by convention, making homology reduced. Dimension of a face $\sigma$ is $\dim \sigma = \mathrm{card}\,\sigma - 1$.
- **Geometric realization $\|\Delta\|$ (9.1, page 1842).** Every finite abstract simplicial complex has a geometric realization in $\mathbb{R}^{2d+1}$, unique up to linear homeomorphism. Topological statements about $\Delta$ mean statements about $\|\Delta\|$.
- **Order complex of a poset (9.3, page 1844).** For a poset $P$, the *order complex* $\Delta(P)$ is the simplicial complex whose faces are the chains (totally ordered subsets) of $P$. This is the central bridge: any poset gives a complex and any complex gives its face poset $P(\Delta)$, and $\Delta(P(\Delta))$ is the barycentric subdivision $\mathrm{sd}\,\Delta$, so $\|\Delta\| \cong \|\mathrm{sd}\,\Delta\| \cong \|\Delta(P(\Delta))\|$ (page 1844). Posets and complexes are topologically interchangeable.
- **Regular cell complex (12.3 to 12.4, pages 1860 to 1861).** A finite CW complex where each closed cell is homeomorphic to a ball and its boundary is a union of lower cells. Key fact (12.4 (ii), page 1861): the order complex of the face poset $\Delta(P(\mathcal{C}))$ is homeomorphic to $\|\mathcal{C}\|$, so a regular cell complex is recoverable as a poset without loss of topological information. This is the precise statement that "the combinatorics determines the topology" for the regular case.
- **Theorem 12.6 (page 1862).** For a finite poset $P$ of rank $d$ with $\Delta(P)$ contractible: if $P \cup \{\hat 0\}$ is thin then it is the face poset of a regular cell complex homotopy equivalent to a wedge of $d$-spheres; if $\hat P$ is thin then $P \cong P(\mathcal{C})$ for a regular cell decomposition of the $d$-sphere.

### Homology, the invariants, and where torsion lives (section 9, "Homotopy and homology", pages 1846 to 1847)
- **Betti numbers and reduced homology (9.11 to 9.13, pages 1846 to 1847).** $\tilde H_i(\Delta, G)$ is reduced simplicial homology with coefficients in an abelian group $G$. The Betti numbers $\beta_i = \dim_\mathbb{Q} H_i(\Delta, \mathbb{Q})$ satisfy the *Euler-Poincare formula* $\sum_{i \ge 0}(-1)^i \mathrm{card}(\Delta^i) = \sum_{i \ge 0}(-1)^i \beta_i$ (formula 9.13, page 1847), so the Euler characteristic is computable from face counts (the f-vector) alone and is independent of coefficient field.
- **Torsion enters through coefficients (9.12, page 1847).** The Kunneth-type decomposition for the join $\tilde H_{i+1}(\Delta_1 * \Delta_2) \cong \bigoplus_{p+q=i}(\tilde H_p(\Delta_1) \otimes \tilde H_q(\Delta_2))$ holds *without restriction* for field coefficients but requires a torsion-free hypothesis on at least one factor when stated over $\mathbb{Z}$. This is the chapter's explicit flag that $\mathbb{Z}$-homology carries information (torsion) invisible to $\mathbb{Q}$ or field coefficients.
- **The acyclicity hierarchy over coefficient rings (11.1, page 1853).** The strict chain of implications:
  $$\text{cone} \Rightarrow \text{nonevasive} \Rightarrow \text{collapsible} \Rightarrow \text{contractible} \Rightarrow \mathbb{Z}\text{-acyclic},$$
  and further
  $$\mathbb{Z}\text{-acyclic} \Rightarrow k\text{-acyclic} \Rightarrow \mathbb{Q}\text{-acyclic} \Rightarrow \tilde\chi = 0.$$
  All implications are strict (converses fail). This is the single most important diagram in the chapter for strand-3: it locates exactly which coefficient ring detects which level of triviality. $\mathbb{Z}/p$-acyclicity (torsion-sensitive) sits strictly above $\mathbb{Q}$-acyclicity, and $\mathbb{Z}/p$-acyclicity for all primes $p$ is what $\mathbb{Z}$-acyclicity unpacks into.
- **Mobius function equals reduced Euler characteristic (9.14, page 1847).** $\mu(x,y) = \tilde\chi(\Delta((x,y)))$ for $x < y$ in a poset: the Mobius function of the open interval equals the reduced Euler characteristic of its order complex (Rota 1964, Folkman 1966). The combinatorial Mobius invariant is exactly the rational Euler characteristic, hence torsion-blind.

### The four named tools the focus asks for

**1. Nerve lemma (Theorem 10.6, page 1850; Theorem 10.7 McCord 1967, page 1851).** Let $\Delta = \bigcup_i \Delta_i$ be covered by subcomplexes. The *nerve* $\mathcal{N}(\{\Delta_i\})$ has a face for each subfamily with nonempty intersection. (i) If every nonempty finite intersection $\Delta_{i_0} \cap \cdots \cap \Delta_{i_t}$ is contractible, then $\Delta$ and its nerve are homotopy equivalent. (ii) A connectivity version: if every such intersection is $(k - t)$-connected then the nerve is $k$-connected iff $\Delta$ is. The continuous-space version (10.7, Weil 1952, McCord 1967) gives homotopy equivalence of a triangulable space and the nerve of a locally finite cover by closed sets with contractible intersections. The crosscut theorem (10.8, page 1851) and the bipartite/neighborhood-complex relation (10.9, page 1851) are corollaries, equivalent to the nerve lemma.

**2. Shellability and the structural hierarchy (sections 11.1 to 11.5, pages 1853 to 1857).**
- **Collapsibility (11.1, page 1853).** An *elementary collapse* removes a free face pair; $\Delta$ is *collapsible* if it collapses to a point. Whitehead: $\Delta$ and $\Delta'$ have the same simple-homotopy type iff a sequence of elementary collapses and anticollapses connects them. Contractible complexes are exactly those that collapse/anticollapse to a point (Cohen 1973).
- **Shellable (11.2, page 1854).** A pure $d$-dimensional complex is *shellable* if its facets can be ordered $\sigma_1, \dots, \sigma_t$ so each $\sigma_k$ meets the union of earlier facets in a pure $(d-1)$-dimensional complex. Lexicographic shellability for order complexes of posets (Bjorner 1980, Bjorner-Wachs 1983) is the practical method.
- **The hierarchy (11.2, page 1854).** For pure $d$-dimensional complexes, the strict implications:
  $$\text{vertex-decomposable} \Rightarrow \text{shellable} \Rightarrow \text{constructible} \Rightarrow (d-1)\text{-connected}.$$
- **Cohen-Macaulay complexes (11.5 to 11.10, pages 1855 to 1857).** $\Delta$ is *Cohen-Macaulay over $k$* (CM/$k$) if $\tilde H_i(\mathrm{lk}_\Delta(\sigma), k) = 0$ for all $i < \dim\mathrm{lk}_\Delta(\sigma)$ and all faces $\sigma$. Theorem (Reisner 1976): $\Delta$ is CM/$k$ iff the Stanley-Reisner face ring $k[\Delta]$ is Cohen-Macaulay as a commutative ring. The strict chain (11.5, page 1855): $\text{constructible} \Rightarrow \text{homotopy-CM} \Rightarrow \text{CM}/\mathbb{Z} \Rightarrow \text{CM}/k \Rightarrow \text{CM}/\mathbb{Q}$, with $\text{CM}/\mathbb{Z} \Leftrightarrow \text{CM}/\mathbb{Z}_p$ for all primes $p$. Crucially (11.5, page 1855): CM-ness over $k$ is *not* a topological invariant when $\mathrm{char}\,k \ne 0$; it depends on the coefficient field. This is a concrete invariant that sees torsion: real projective $d$-space triangulations are CM/$\mathbb{Q}$ but not CM/$\mathbb{Z}_2$ (page 1856).

**3. $\mathbb{Z}/2$-index and Borsuk-Ulam (sections 4, 5, 13, pages 1825 to 1829, 1864 to 1865).**
- **Borsuk's theorem (Theorem 13.6, page 1864).** Five equivalent forms. (ii) For continuous $f: S^d \to \mathbb{R}^d$ there is $x$ with $f(x) = f(-x)$. (iv) There is *no* equivariant map $S^m \to S^d$ if $m > d$. (v) For any $d$-connected antipodality space (a $\mathbb{Z}/2$-space, i.e. a free involution) $T$, there is no equivariant map $T \to S^d$. The $\mathbb{Z}/2$-index machinery: connectivity of a $\mathbb{Z}/2$-complex obstructs equivariant maps to low-dimensional spheres.
- **Kneser's conjecture (Theorem 4.1, Lovasz 1978, page 1825).** The $n$-subsets of a $(2n+k)$-set, partitioned into $k+1$ classes, force a class with two disjoint $n$-sets. Proof via Theorem 4.2: if the *neighborhood complex* $\mathcal{N}(G)$ of a graph $G$ is $(k-1)$-connected then $G$ is not $(k+1)$-colorable. The neighborhood complex of the Kneser graph is shown $(k-1)$-connected via shellability of a crosscut complex (page 1826) and a Borsuk-Ulam equivariant-map obstruction $\bar{\mathcal{N}}(G) \simeq S^{k-1}$ (page 1827). This is the archetypal $\mathbb{Z}/2$-index argument: a combinatorial coloring lower bound from the topological connectivity of an associated complex.
- **Ham-sandwich and Tverberg (Theorems 5.1 to 5.3, 13.7 to 13.8, pages 1828 to 1829, 1865).** Borsuk-Ulam gives simultaneous bisection (ham-sandwich, 13.7) and partition theorems. The topological Tverberg theorem (5.3, Zivaljevic-Vrecica 1992) uses the non-existence of an equivariant map for free $\mathbb{Z}_p$-actions, $p$ prime: the deleted-join construction and $\mathbb{Z}_p$ (not just $\mathbb{Z}/2$) index. The colored Tverberg proof (page 1827, 1829) needs $t$ prime, an explicit place where the prime $p$ (mod-$p$ torsion) is structurally load-bearing.

**4. Lefschetz fixed-point counting (section 13, pages 1862 to 1864).**
- **Lefschetz number via the Hopf trace formula (page 1862).** For a self-map $f: \|\Delta\| \to \|\Delta\|$, the *Lefschetz number* is $\Lambda(f) = \sum_{i \ge 0}(-1)^i \mathrm{trace}(f_*^i)$ where $f_*^i: \tilde H_i(\Delta, \mathbb{Q}) \to \tilde H_i(\Delta, \mathbb{Q})$ (rational coefficients used for simplicity). The Hopf trace formula computes $\Lambda(f)$ alternatively as an alternating sum of traces on chain groups: for a simplicial $f$,
  $$\Lambda(f) + 1 = \sum_{i \ge 0}(-1)^i |\varphi_i^*(f)|$$
  where $\varphi_i^*(f)$ counts the fixed $i$-faces whose orientation is preserved (formula 13.2, page 1863). For $f = \mathrm{id}$ this specializes to the Euler-Poincare formula (9.13).
- **Theorem 13.3 (Lefschetz, page 1863).** If $\Lambda(f) \ne -1$ then $f$ has a fixed face. Corollary 13.4: every self-map of a $\mathbb{Q}$-acyclic compact triangulable space has a fixed point. For an order-reversing $f$ on a poset, formula 13.5 rewrites $\Lambda(f) = \mu(\hat 0, \hat 1)$ augmented (page 1864): the Lefschetz number of a poset map becomes a Mobius-function value.
- **Lefschetz fixed-point property for posets (Theorem 3.1, Baclawski-Bjorner 1979, page 1824).** A finite lattice $L$ with $z \in \bar L$ such that $\bar L - \mathscr{C}o(z)$ has the fixed-point property; via Theorem 10.15 the order complex is contractible hence has the topological fixed-point property. The Hopf trace formula (13.5) forces $\mu(P^f) = 0$ for any $\mathbb{Q}$-acyclic poset $P$: the Mobius value of the fixed-point subposet vanishes.

### Evasiveness (section 2, pages 1822 to 1823): the topological lower-bound prototype
- **Theorem 2.1 (Kahn-Saks-Sturtevant 1984, page 1823).** Let $n = p^k$, $p$ prime. Then every non-trivial monotone property of graphs on $n$ vertices is *evasive* (decision-tree complexity exactly $\binom{n}{2}$). The proof (sketched, pages 1822 to 1823): a non-evasive monotone property gives a *collapsible* (hence $\mathbb{Z}_p$-acyclic, hence contractible) simplicial complex $\Delta_{\mathscr{P}}$; one constructs a group $\Gamma$ of simplicial automorphisms acting on $\Delta_{\mathscr{P}}$ with a normal $p$-subgroup $\Gamma_1$ such that $\Gamma/\Gamma_1$ is cyclic; by Oliver's theorem (1975) any such action on a $\mathbb{Z}_p$-acyclic complex has a fixed point. The fixed point must lie in the relative interior of a $\Gamma$-invariant face, but transitivity of $\Gamma$ on $\binom{V}{2}$ forces that face to be the whole simplex, contradicting non-triviality. Yao (1988) extended this to bipartite graphs by the topological method.

### Oriented matroids and the topological representation theorem (section 7, pages 1834 to 1838)
- **Theorem 7.1 (Folkman-Lawrence 1978, page 1837).** Rank $d$ oriented matroids correspond bijectively (up to relabeling) to arrangements of pseudospheres in $S^{d-1}$, equivalently to regular cell decompositions of the sphere by pseudohyperplanes. The proof constructs a poset from the oriented matroid and shows (via Theorem 12.6) that it is the face poset of a regular cell complex. This is the chapter's example (iii): for oriented matroids the topology is not subordinate but *is* the object, and combinatorial properties get clean topological proofs (e.g. the determinantal sign function, Theorem 7.6, Las Vergnas 1978, page 1838).

### Hard Lefschetz and f-vectors (section 8, pages 1839 to 1841)
- **Theorem 8.1 (Hard Lefschetz, page 1839).** For a smooth irreducible complex projective variety $X$ of dimension $d$ and $\omega \in H^2(X)$, multiplication by $\omega^{d-i}: H^i(X) \to H^{2d-i}(X)$ is an isomorphism. Stanley (1980a) used this on toric varieties $X(P)$ of simplicial polytopes $P$ to prove the necessity half of the g-theorem (the characterization of f-vectors of simplicial polytopes, the Erdos-Moser / McMullen conjecture). The $h$-vector becomes $\dim_\mathbb{R} H^{2i}(X(P)) = h_i$ (page 1841), and Hard Lefschetz forces the unimodality / M-sequence conditions. McMullen (1993) later gave a proof replacing the cohomology ring by a polytope-algebra construction, avoiding algebraic geometry. *This proof is essentially the use of real ($\mathbb{R}$-coefficient) cohomology multiplicative structure; it is torsion-free by design (page 1840 notes $H^i(X)$ and $H^{2d-i}(X)$ are isomorphic away from Poincare duality, a purely multiplicative phenomenon).*

## Techniques and proof ideas

The reusable kit a BUILDER would extract, organized by what it computes:

1. **Poset $\to$ complex $\to$ topology pipeline (9.3, 12.4).** Encode a combinatorial object as a poset, take the order complex, read off homotopy type / connectivity / homology. The whole subject runs on this functor. For any combinatorially defined complex, $\|\Delta\| \cong \|\Delta(P(\Delta))\|$ means the invariant computation reduces to chains in a poset.

2. **Nerve lemma as a divide-and-conquer (10.6).** To compute the homotopy type of a complex, cover it by pieces with contractible (or highly connected) intersections and replace it by the combinatorially simpler nerve. This is the workhorse for connectivity lower bounds (the Kneser application runs through it).

3. **Fiber / quotient homotopy theorems (Theorem 10.5 Quillen-Walker, 10.10, 10.11, pages 1849 to 1851).** For a simplicial map $f: \Delta \to P$ to a poset, if all fibers $f^{-1}(P_{\le x})$ are contractible then $f$ is a homotopy equivalence (10.5 (i)); if all fibers are $k$-connected then $\Delta$ is $k$-connected iff $P$ is (10.5 (ii)). The Order Homotopy Theorem (10.11): if $f(x) \le g(x)$ pointwise for poset maps then $f \simeq g$. These convert "is this complex highly connected" into local fiber checks.

4. **Shelling and lexicographic shellability (11.2).** To prove a complex is a wedge of spheres (and compute its Betti numbers and that it is CM), exhibit a shelling order of facets. For order complexes, use EL-labelings. Shellability gives Cohen-Macaulayness, hence the Reisner ring-theoretic consequences.

5. **Borsuk-Ulam / $\mathbb{Z}/2$-index obstruction (13.6).** To prove a combinatorial lower bound (chromatic number, partition existence), build a free $\mathbb{Z}/2$-space (or $\mathbb{Z}_p$-space) whose high connectivity contradicts the existence of an equivariant map to a low sphere. The deleted-join construction is the standard gadget for raising the symmetry to $\mathbb{Z}_p$.

6. **Hopf trace / Lefschetz counting (13.2, 13.3).** To prove a fixed point or fixed face must exist, compute the Lefschetz number as an alternating trace sum; nonzero (more precisely $\ne -1$) forces a fixed face. For poset self-maps this is a Mobius computation (13.5). The Kahn-Saks-Sturtevant evasiveness proof is the headline application: Oliver's fixed-point theorem on $\mathbb{Z}_p$-acyclic complexes plus a group action.

7. **Stanley-Reisner ring + Hard Lefschetz (8.1, 11.5).** To prove enumerative inequalities (f-vector / g-theorem), pass to the face ring or toric variety cohomology and invoke the multiplicative structure (Hard Lefschetz isomorphism). This couples commutative algebra, algebraic geometry, and combinatorics.

## Relevance to this project

The strand-3 hypothesis is that the program's "missing object" is a *non-algebrizing, fast-computable invariant*, and the speculative candidate is a mod-2 torsion ("Bockstein bridge") quantity. The algebrization barrier (Aaronson-Wigderson 2008) covers techniques that go through low-degree polynomial / rational extensions; the LEARNINGS finding (9) is that *every rational trace / rank / volume invariant algebrizes*, so the wanted object must be something a fast counting algorithm computes that is genuinely not a rational/algebraic quantity. Torsion is the natural place to look, because torsion is precisely the part of $\mathbb{Z}$-homology that is invisible to $\mathbb{Q}$ (rational) coefficients.

This chapter is the map of that territory. Its concrete contributions to the strand:

- **It pins exactly which combinatorial-complex invariants are rational (hence likely algebrizing) and which see torsion.** The Euler characteristic, Betti numbers over $\mathbb{Q}$, and the Mobius function (9.13, 9.14) are all rational and field-independent: these are the algebrizing-flavored invariants. The torsion-sensitive invariants are: $\mathbb{Z}$-homology vs $\mathbb{Q}$-homology (9.12), the strict gap between $\mathbb{Z}/p$-acyclic and $\mathbb{Q}$-acyclic in the hierarchy (11.1, page 1853), and Cohen-Macaulayness over $\mathbb{Z}_p$ vs $\mathbb{Q}$ (11.5, page 1855, which is explicitly *not* a topological invariant in positive characteristic). The real-projective-space example (CM/$\mathbb{Q}$ but not CM/$\mathbb{Z}_2$, page 1856) is the cleanest finite combinatorial witness that mod-2 torsion carries information no rational invariant sees. This is exactly the kind of object the "Bockstein bridge" idea wants.

- **The evasiveness proof (Theorem 2.1, page 1823) is the closest thing in the chapter to a complexity lower bound, and it is essentially torsion-driven.** It works only for $n = p^k$ and uses $\mathbb{Z}_p$-acyclicity (Oliver's theorem), not $\mathbb{Q}$-acyclicity. The dependence on the prime $p$ is structural, not cosmetic: the argument fails for general $n$ precisely because $\mathbb{Q}$-acyclicity is too weak. This is a documented case where a *mod-$p$ / torsion* topological invariant yields a query-complexity lower bound that the rational invariant cannot. It is the template the strand should study: a fast-checkable combinatorial property (evasiveness) controlled by a torsion-sensitive topological obstruction (fixed points on $\mathbb{Z}_p$-acyclic complexes).

- **The colored Tverberg / topological Tverberg proofs (5.3, page 1829) need $p$ prime for the same reason.** Free $\mathbb{Z}_p$-actions and the prime hypothesis are load-bearing; the deleted-join $\mathbb{Z}_p$-index argument has no rational substitute. Another data point that the interesting combinatorial-topology lower bounds are torsion/prime-indexed.

On the barrier discipline directly: this is a *source of candidate invariants*, not itself a candidate technique, so it does not get run through the three-barrier checker as-is. The honest classification: the rational invariants this chapter computes (Euler characteristic, $\mathbb{Q}$-Betti numbers, Mobius function, Lefschetz number over $\mathbb{Q}$) are exactly the algebrizing-flavored quantities; the torsion invariants ($\mathbb{Z}/p$-homology, CM/$\mathbb{Z}_p$, the $\mathbb{Z}_p$-index) are the ones whose non-algebrization is plausible but *unproven*. The chapter says nothing about whether any of these is *fast-computable* in the complexity sense, which is the gap a BUILDER and ADVERSARY must close.

## What this enables / what remains open

Mapping to the research directions:
- **01 (circuit lower bounds, Williams ACC0 toward P/poly):** indirect. The evasiveness theorem is a decision-tree (query) lower bound, not a circuit lower bound, but it is the cleanest worked example of a topological lower-bound argument that is genuinely torsion-driven and prime-indexed. A builder pursuing the Williams algorithm-to-lower-bound spine should treat it as a proof-of-concept that topological obstructions can produce concrete complexity lower bounds (finding 6: barriers are passable, here demonstrated in the query model).
- **02 (natural-proofs evasion, the MCSP crux):** speculative but on-point. The torsion invariants here ($\mathbb{Z}_p$-acyclicity, CM/$\mathbb{Z}_2$) are not obviously "large" (they need not hold for most functions) and their constructivity is unknown. If a topological torsion invariant could be shown *not* large or *not* constructive, it would be a candidate natural-proofs-evading property. This chapter supplies the menu; it does not evaluate naturalness.
- **03 (proof complexity, NP vs coNP):** marginal. No direct connection in the text.
- **04 (GCT):** tangential. The Hard Lefschetz / Stanley-Reisner / toric-variety machinery (section 8) is representation-theory-and-algebraic-geometry flavored, the same neighborhood as GCT, and the chapter notes (page 1841) that *multiplicity* and intersection cohomology (Goresky-MacPherson) recover combinatorial control for non-simplicial polytopes. This resonates with finding (7) (GCT hit its occurrence-obstruction no-go and multiplicity obstructions are the live remnant): multiplicity-vs-occurrence is exactly the distinction that appears when cohomology over $\mathbb{Z}$ degenerates.

Mapping to LEARNINGS findings:
- Sharpens **(9) the missing object is a non-algebrizing fast-computable invariant.** The chapter gives a precise inventory: rational invariants (Euler characteristic, $\mathbb{Q}$-Betti, Mobius, $\mathbb{Q}$-Lefschetz) are the algebrizing ones; torsion invariants ($\mathbb{Z}/p$-homology, CM/$\mathbb{Z}_p$, $\mathbb{Z}_p$-index) are the non-rational candidates. The Bockstein-bridge intuition is well-founded in the sense that the Bockstein homomorphism is exactly the operator detecting the mod-$p$ torsion that distinguishes $\mathbb{Z}/p$ from $\mathbb{Q}$ homology (the chapter does not name the Bockstein, but its torsion-vs-rational hierarchy at 11.1 is the home of that operator).
- Supports **(6) Williams is the existence proof barriers are passable** by analogy: the evasiveness theorem shows a torsion-topological obstruction yielding a tight complexity (query) lower bound that no rational/counting argument reaches.
- Touches **(7) GCT multiplicity obstructions** via the Hard Lefschetz / intersection-cohomology remark (page 1841).

Concrete open questions and cruxes this source exposes:
1. **Is any torsion invariant of a combinatorially presented complex fast-computable?** The chapter establishes that $\mathbb{Z}/p$-homology and CM/$\mathbb{Z}_p$ see torsion that $\mathbb{Q}$ misses, but says nothing about computational complexity. The strand's whole bet hinges on this: a Bockstein-type invariant is only useful as a "missing object" if a fast (#SAT-style) algorithm can compute it. Determine the complexity of computing $\dim_{\mathbb{Z}_p} H_i(\Delta, \mathbb{Z}_p)$ vs $\dim_\mathbb{Q} H_i(\Delta, \mathbb{Q})$ for a succinctly presented $\Delta$.
2. **Does the evasiveness/Oliver template generalize from query complexity to circuit complexity?** The Kahn-Saks-Sturtevant argument (2.1) gives a clean torsion-driven lower bound in the decision-tree model. Identify whether the $\mathbb{Z}_p$-acyclicity + group-action obstruction has any analog for circuit size, where the relevant complex would encode circuits rather than graphs.
3. **Reconcile "fast-computable" with "non-algebrizing."** Every invariant in this chapter that is known to be efficiently computable (Euler characteristic via f-vector, Mobius via interval enumeration) is rational and presumably algebrizes. The torsion invariants are not known to be efficiently computable. The crux for ADVERSARY: is this a fundamental tension (anything torsion-detecting is hard to compute, anything fast is rational) or an unexplored gap?
4. **Pin the Bockstein bridge.** The chapter motivates but does not contain the Bockstein homomorphism. Follow-up read: a source that treats the Bockstein $\beta: H^i(\Delta, \mathbb{Z}/p) \to H^{i+1}(\Delta, \mathbb{Z})$ and its computability (Hatcher 2002, already in `references/algebraic_topology/`, is the natural next read). The Miller 2013 source in the same directory (`miller_2013_evasiveness_topological_fixed_point.pdf`) is the direct modern follow-up on the evasiveness/fixed-point connection and should be read next.
5. **Follow-up references from the chapter's bibliography (pages 1866 to 1872):** Kahn-Saks-Sturtevant 1984 (evasiveness, *Combinatorica*); Oliver 1975 (*Comment. Math. Helv.*, fixed points of group actions on acyclic complexes, the torsion engine); Reisner 1976 (CM rings, the algebra-topology bridge); Lovasz 1978 (Kneser, *J. Combin. Theory A*); Folkman-Lawrence 1978 (oriented-matroid representation); Stanley 1980a (Hard Lefschetz and the g-theorem); Bjorner-Wachs 1983 (lexicographic shellability); Bjorner 1992 (matroids and CM, in *Matroid Applications*). For strand-3 the Oliver 1975 and Kahn-Saks-Sturtevant 1984 pair is the priority: they are the documented case of a torsion (mod-$p$) topological invariant delivering a complexity lower bound.
