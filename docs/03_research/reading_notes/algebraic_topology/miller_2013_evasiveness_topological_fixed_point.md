## Summary
This is a fully self-contained tutorial on the topological method for proving graph properties evasive, built around the 1984 paper of Kahn, Saks, and Sturtevant (KSS, reference [18] in the survey). The central translation: a monotone-increasing graph property $h$ has an associated abstract simplicial complex $\Delta_h$, and if $h$ is *not* evasive then $\Delta_h$ is collapsible (Theorem 2.1), hence $\mathbb{F}_p$-acyclic (Theorem 3.6), hence (via a fixed-point theorem) admits forced fixed points under suitable group actions. Choosing a vertex-transitive automorphism group with no nontrivial invariant graph then contradicts nonevasiveness. The monograph develops all the algebraic topology from scratch (chain complexes, $\mathbb{F}_p$-homology, the Lefschetz fixed-point theorem, and R. Oliver's nonabelian fixed-point theorem) and applies it to prove three capstone lower bounds: KSS evasiveness for prime-power vertex sets (Theorem 5.1), Yao's evasiveness of bipartite graph properties (Theorem 5.3), and the general $D(h) \ge n^2/4 - o(n^2)$ bound (Theorem 5.5).

## Key definitions and results

### The decision-tree model and evasiveness (Chapter 1, Chapter 2)
- **Graph property** (eq 1.1, eq 2.5): an isomorphism-invariant function $f : \mathbf{G}(V) \to \{0,1\}$ on graphs over a fixed finite vertex set $V$, $|V| = n$.
- **Decision-tree complexity** $D(f)$ (Introduction, p 1; section 2.1): the least depth of an adaptive binary decision tree that queries individual edges $\{i,j\}$ and computes $f$. The trivial upper bound is $D(h) \le \binom{n}{2}$ (eq 2.9).
- **Evasive** (eq 2.10): $f$ is evasive if $D(f) = \binom{n}{2}$, the maximum. Equivalently every edge must be queried in the worst case.
- **Monotone** (p 2, section 2.1): $h$ is monotone-increasing if adding edges never destroys the property ($h(G') \le h(G)$ whenever $G' \subseteq G$); monotone-decreasing is the dual. The "has a cycle" property is the introductory adversary example proving such properties can be evasive (Introduction, p 1 to 2).
- **The Karp conjecture (Conjecture 1.1, p 2):** all nontrivial monotone graph properties are evasive. Open. Aanderaa-Rosenberg conjectured the weaker $D(f) = \Omega(n^2)$; Rivest-Vuillemin proved $D(f) \ge n^2/16$, improved by Kleitman-Kwiatkowski to $n^2/9$ (section 1.1, p 3). The topological method gives the current best general constant.

### The complex of a property, and the collapsibility bridge (Chapter 2)
- **Abstract simplicial complex** (Definition 2.4): a downward-closed family $\Delta$ of nonempty finite sets. Dimension of a simplex $Q$ is $|Q| - 1$ (Definition 2.5).
- **The associated complex $\Delta_h$** (eq 2.17, section 2.3): for monotone-increasing $h$ on $V_0$, $\Delta_h$ is the set of nonempty edge-sets $E$ with $h((V_0,E)) = 0$. Crucially, the **vertices of $\Delta_h$ are edges of the graph, not vertices of the graph**, and simplices of $\Delta_h$ are the non-$h$ graphs. Monotone-increasing $h$ implies $\Delta_h$ is downward closed, i.e. genuinely a complex (eq 2.15). Example: "connectedness" on four vertices is drawn as an octahedron-like complex in $\mathbb{R}^3$ (Fig 1.2); the complex of "vertex 2 has an edge" is a solid triangle (Fig 2.8).
- **Free face, elementary collapse, collapsible** (Definitions 2.7, 2.8, 2.9): a free face is a nonmaximal simplex contained in exactly one maximal simplex; an elementary collapse deletes a free face and everything containing it; $\Delta$ is **collapsible** if a sequence of elementary collapses reduces it to a single point ($|\Delta_n| = 1$). Collapsibility is the discrete, combinatorial form of contractibility.
- **Theorem 2.1 (the key bridge, p 19):** if $h$ is monotone-increasing and *not* evasive, and $\Delta_h$ is nonempty, then $\Delta_h$ is collapsible. Proof idea (visual, attributed to Yaoyun Shi): a sub-maximal-depth decision tree for $h$ is regularized to a uniform-height tree $T'$ of height $\lceil n(n-1)/2 - 1\rceil$ with no repeated edges on any path (Figs 2.10 to 2.12). The leaves get a total order (Y-branches before N-branches). Each "0"-leaf corresponds to a pair $\gamma_1 \subseteq \gamma_2$ of simplices in $\Delta_h$ that are free at the moment they are processed, so reading the leaves in order yields an explicit collapsing sequence (eqs 2.28 to 2.30). This is the direction that matters: nonevasiveness is a *strong* structural constraint (collapsibility), not merely the weak even-odd weight-balance / divisibility-by-$(1+t)^k$ condition of Rivest-Vuillemin (section 1.1, p 3).

### Group actions and the invariant complex (section 2.4)
- **Simplicial automorphism** (Definition 2.10, eqs 2.34 to 2.35): an inclusion-preserving permutation of the simplices, equivalently a vertex permutation $b$ with $Q \in \Delta \Rightarrow b(Q) \in \Delta$. A **group action** $G \circlearrowright \Delta$ is an action by simplicial automorphisms.
- The naive fixed-point set $\Delta^G$ (invariant simplices) is **not always a subcomplex** (counterexample p 23: the trapezoid $\Sigma$ with the swap $\{0\}\leftrightarrow\{2\}$). The fix is **$\Delta^{[G]}$** (Definition 2.11, eq 2.38): take orbits $A_1,\dots,A_m$ of $G$ on the vertex set and let $\Delta^{[G]}$ be the subsets $T \subseteq \{A_1,\dots,A_m\}$ whose union of orbits lies in $\Delta$. $\Delta^{[G]}$ is always a complex. This distinction ($\Delta^G$ vs $\Delta^{[G]}$, reconciled later by barycentric subdivision) is a recurring technical subtlety.

### Chain complexes, $\mathbb{F}_p$-homology, and acyclicity (Chapter 3)
- **Chain group** $K_n(\Delta, \mathbb{F}_p)$ (Definition 3.1, 3.3): formal $\mathbb{F}_p$-linear combinations of (ordered) $n$-simplices. **Boundary map** $d_n([v_0,\dots,v_n]) = \sum_{i=0}^n (-1)^i [v_0,\dots,\hat{v_i},\dots,v_n]$ (eq 3.11, 3.24), with $d_{n-1} \circ d_n = 0$ (Proposition 3.2, proof by index cancellation eq 3.20). **Homology** $H_n(\Delta,\mathbb{F}_p) = \ker d_n / \operatorname{im} d_{n+1}$ (eq 3.22). $\dim H_0$ equals the number of connected components; $\dim H_1$ over $\mathbb{R}^2$ equals the number of enclosed holes (section 3.3, eqs 3.42 to 3.44, with torus/annulus pictures Figs 3.1 to 3.4). The choice of coefficients $\mathbb{F}_p$ (not $\mathbb{R}$) is deliberate: Proposition 4.4 (Smith theory) needs $\mathbb{F}_p$ critically (note p 30).
- **Reduced homology and $\mathbb{F}_p$-acyclicity** (Definitions 3.5, 3.6): augment by $s : K_0 \to \mathbb{F}_p$ (sum of coefficients) to get reduced groups $\tilde{H}_n$. $\Delta$ is **$\mathbb{F}_p$-acyclic** if all $\tilde{H}_n(\Delta,\mathbb{F}_p) = 0$, i.e. its $\mathbb{F}_p$-homology is that of a point.
- **Theorem 3.6 (collapsible $\Rightarrow$ $\mathbb{F}_p$-acyclic, p 42):** every collapsible complex is $\mathbb{F}_p$-acyclic for every prime $p$. Proof: reduce to *primitive* elementary collapses, each of which changes the chain complex by a quotient $Z_\bullet$ isomorphic to $\dots \to 0 \to \mathbb{F}_p \xrightarrow{\mathrm{Id}} \mathbb{F}_p \to 0 \to \dots$ (eq 3.71), which is exact; Lemma 3.5 (the trace/exactness algebra, built on the long exact sequence Proposition 3.4) then propagates acyclicity by induction. Chaining: not evasive $\Rightarrow$ collapsible (Thm 2.1) $\Rightarrow$ $\mathbb{F}_p$-acyclic (Thm 3.6).

### Fixed-point theorems (Chapter 4) - the obstruction engine
- **Theorem 4.1 (Lefschetz fixed-point theorem, p 45):** if $\Delta$ is finite and $\mathbb{F}_p$-acyclic and $f : \Delta \to \Delta$ is a simplicial automorphism, then some simplex $Q$ satisfies $f(Q) = Q$. Proof: the trace function $\operatorname{Tr}$ is additive on exact sequences (eq 4.2). If $f$ fixed no simplex, then on each chain group $K_n$ the induced map $F$ permutes basis simplices with no fixed points, forcing $\operatorname{Tr}_F(K_n) = 0$, so $\sum_n (-1)^n \operatorname{Tr}_F(K_n) = 0$. But the Hopf trace formula rewrites this as $\sum_n (-1)^n \operatorname{Tr}_F(H_n)$, which acyclicity makes equal to $1$ (the trivial action on $H_0 \cong \mathbb{F}_p$). Contradiction. This is the precise sense in which **a nonzero topological invariant (the Lefschetz number / Euler characteristic $= 1$) forces the existence of a fixed simplex.**
- **Geometric reading (p 47):** a fixed simplex contains an affine-linearly fixed barycenter $\sum_i \frac{1}{n+1}\mathbf{v}_i$ (eq 4.7), justifying the name "fixed point." The Euler characteristic $\chi(S) = \sum_n (-1)^n |\{Q \in S : \dim Q = n\}|$ (eq 4.10), and Theorem 4.3 gives $\chi(\Delta^f) = 1$ for acyclic $\Delta$.
- **Proposition 4.4 (Smith theory, p 49):** if $\Delta$ is $\mathbb{F}_p$-acyclic and $f$ is an order-$p$ automorphism with $\Delta^f$ a subcomplex, then $\Delta^f$ is itself $\mathbb{F}_p$-acyclic. Proof follows P. A. Smith [35]: define $\delta = \mathbb{I} - F$ and $\sigma = \mathbb{I} + F + \dots + F^{p-1}$ on chains (eqs 4.16 to 4.18), exhibit explicit bases for the four subcomplexes $\operatorname{im}\delta, \ker\delta, \operatorname{im}\sigma, \ker\sigma$ (p 50), giving isomorphisms $(\ker\sigma) \cong (\operatorname{im}\delta) \oplus K_\bullet(\Delta^f)$ and $(\ker\delta) \cong (\operatorname{im}\sigma) \oplus K_\bullet(\Delta^f)$ (eqs 4.20, 4.21); the long exact sequences then dimension-count to force $\Delta^f$ acyclic (eqs 4.22 to 4.28). This is the one place $\mathbb{F}_p$ coefficients are essential.
- **Corollary 4.5 (p 52):** any $p$-group $H$ of order $p^m$ acting with each $\Delta^h$ a subcomplex has $\Delta^H$ $\mathbb{F}_p$-acyclic (induction up a normal filtration with $\mathbb{Z}/p$ quotients, eqs 4.29 to 4.30).
- **Theorem 4.6 / Theorem 1.2 (R. Oliver's theorem, p 53):** if a finite group $G$ has a normal subgroup $G'$ of prime-power order with $G/G'$ cyclic, and $G$ acts on a collapsible $\Delta$ with each $\Delta^g$ a subcomplex, then $\chi(\Delta^G) = 1$; in particular $\Delta^G \ne \emptyset$. Proof: $\Delta^{G'}$ is $\mathbb{F}_p$-acyclic (Cor 4.5), then a generator $b$ of the cyclic $G/G'$ acts on $\Delta^{G'}$ and Lefschetz (Thm 4.1) forces $(\Delta^{G'})^b = \Delta^G$ to have $\chi = 1$.
- **Barycentric subdivision (section 4.3)** removes the unnatural hypothesis "$\Delta^g$ is a subcomplex," which fails for graph complexes. $\operatorname{bar}(\Delta)$ (Definition 4.1) is the complex of $\subset$-chains in $\Delta$; it is collapsible if $\Delta$ is (Prop 4.7), preserves Euler characteristic (Prop 4.8), and always satisfies $(\operatorname{bar}\Delta)^g$ a subcomplex (Lemma 4.9). This yields **Theorem 4.10:** collapsible $\Delta$ with $G$ as in Oliver's theorem has $\Delta^G \ne \emptyset$ unconditionally, and **Theorem 4.11:** $\chi(\Delta^{[G]}) = 1$ via the isomorphism $\operatorname{bar}(\Delta^{[G]}) \cong \operatorname{bar}(\Delta)^G$ (eq 4.36).

### The capstone lower bounds (Chapter 5)
- **Theorem 5.1 / Theorem 1.3 (KSS, prime-power order, p 60):** every nontrivial monotone-increasing graph property on $|V| = p^k$ vertices is evasive. Proof: identify $V = \mathbb{F}_{p^k}$ and let $G$ be the affine group $x \mapsto ax + b$ ($a \ne 0$), with $G' = \{x \mapsto x+b\}$ the additive (elementary abelian, order $p^k$) normal subgroup, and $G/G'$ the cyclic multiplicative group $\mathbb{F}_{p^k}^*$ (observations (1) to (4), eqs 5.5 to 5.8). $G$ is transitive on ordered pairs of distinct vertices, so the only nonempty $G$-invariant graph is the complete graph. If $h$ were nonevasive, $\Delta_h$ would be collapsible, Theorem 4.10 forces a nonempty $G$-invariant non-$h$ graph, which must be complete, making $h$ trivial. Contradiction.
- **Theorem 5.3 (Yao, bipartite, p 63):** every nontrivial monotone-increasing bipartite graph property is evasive (i.e. $D(f) = |Y| \cdot |Z|$). Proof uses a cyclic action on one side $Y$; $G$-invariant graphs are "stars" $H_S$, the invariant complex is determined by subsets of $Z$, and a direct Euler-characteristic computation (eqs 5.18, 5.19) combined with $\chi(\Delta^{[G]}) = 1$ (Thm 4.11) forces triviality unless $k = |Z|$.
- **Proposition 5.4 / Theorem 5.5 / Theorem 1.4 (general bound, p 64 to 66):** for arbitrary $n$, letting $p$ be the largest prime $\le n$, $D(h) \ge p^2/4$ (Prop 5.4, eqs 5.22 to 5.28, by an inductive case analysis on $h(K_1)$, $h(K_{1,n-1})$, $h(K_{n-1})$ reducing to a smaller property or a bipartite one). The prime number theorem then gives $C_n \ge n^2/4 - o(n^2)$ (Theorem 5.5, eqs 5.29 to 5.31). The dependence on prime gaps is genuine: this is why the bound is stated for $C_n$ via the largest prime below $n$.

### Survey of stronger bounds (section 5.4, stated without proof)
- King [19]: directed monotone properties have $C'_n \ge n^2/2 - o(n^2)$ (Theorem 5.6).
- Korneffel and Triesch [23]: improved the undirected constant to $C_n \ge 8n^2/25 - o(n^2)$ (Theorem 5.7) via a tripartite $\mathbb{Z}/p \times \mathbb{Z}/p \times \mathbb{Z}/(n-2p)$ action (eq 5.33).
- Chakrabarti, Khot, Shi [8]: subgraph-containment property $h_{X,n}$ is evasive on a positive-density set of $n$, with $D(h_{X,n}) \ge n^2/2 - O(n)$ (eq 5.35).
- Babai et al [3]: $h_{X,n}$ is evasive for almost all $n$, with $D(h_{X,n}) \ge \binom{n}{2} - O(1)$ (eq 5.36). The author closes (p 68) noting that proof difficulty has climbed steeply and that "perhaps a new basic insight, like the one in [18], will be necessary to proceed further toward the Karp conjecture."

## Techniques and proof ideas
The reusable template (the thing a BUILDER would lift) is a four-step obstruction pipeline:

1. **Encode the hardness question as a complex.** Map the monotone property $h$ to $\Delta_h$ whose vertices are *edge slots* and whose simplices are the "no" instances (non-$h$ graphs). This is the functor $h \mapsto \Delta_h$ (section 2.3).
2. **Turn the negation of hardness into contractibility.** Nonevasiveness $\Rightarrow$ collapsibility (Theorem 2.1), read off directly from a shallow decision tree. The decision tree *is* the collapsing schedule. This is the bridge between an algorithmic object (the tree) and a topological one (the collapse).
3. **Convert contractibility into a homological vanishing.** Collapsible $\Rightarrow$ $\mathbb{F}_p$-acyclic (Theorem 3.6). Now the only surviving invariant is $H_0 \cong \mathbb{F}_p$, so the Lefschetz/Euler number is pinned to $1$.
4. **Use a forced nonzero invariant to manufacture a contradiction.** A carefully chosen vertex-transitive group action (affine group for prime-power $n$; cyclic for bipartite/Yao; tripartite abelian for Korneffel-Triesch) has *no* nontrivial invariant graph except the complete graph. The fixed-point theorem (Lefschetz, then Oliver via Smith theory and barycentric subdivision) forces such an invariant to exist. Triviality of $h$ follows, contradicting nontriviality.

The deepest reusable machinery: (a) the Hopf trace formula computing the Lefschetz number as an alternating sum over chain groups equal to the alternating sum over homology; (b) Smith theory (Proposition 4.4), the $\mathbb{F}_p$-specific result that $p$-group fixed-point sets inherit acyclicity, proved via the operators $\delta = \mathbb{I}-F$, $\sigma = \sum F^i$; (c) barycentric subdivision as the device that makes fixed-point sets into honest subcomplexes (Lemma 4.9), letting the abstract fixed-point theorems apply to combinatorially-defined group actions. The recurring obstacle the author handles repeatedly: the symmetric group $\operatorname{Sym}(V)$ is "too big" for any fixed-point theorem proved here (p 60), so the art is choosing a subgroup large enough to kill invariants but structured enough (prime-power-times-cyclic) to satisfy Oliver's hypothesis.

## Relevance to this project
This monograph is the canonical, end-to-end worked instance of **topology forcing a complexity lower bound** without arithmetization, without circuit-size counting, and without a natural-proofs-style largeness/constructivity property. That makes it directly load-bearing for the strand-3 "missing object" search, which is hunting for a *non-algebrizing, fast-computable invariant* with a torsion/Bockstein flavor.

- **It is a non-natural certificate.** The evasiveness lower bound does not proceed by exhibiting a large, easily-computable family of hard functions. It proceeds by a topological *obstruction*: the complex $\Delta_h$ fails to be acyclic (equivalently fails to be collapsible), and that single homological fact certifies hardness of one specific property. This is structurally the opposite of a Razborov-Rudich natural property, which aligns with LEARNINGS finding (2) "natural proofs are common" by being a clear example of a proof that is *not* natural. It is a per-instance obstruction, not a measure on all functions.
- **It is the cleanest precedent for "a topological invariant certifies decision hardness."** The dossier's missing object is described as a mod-2 torsion "Bockstein bridge," a quantity computed by a fast algorithm that does not algebrize because every rational trace/rank/volume invariant algebrizes. Miller's proof is suggestive precisely because its decisive invariant lives in $\mathbb{F}_p$-homology (Theorem 3.6, Proposition 4.4) and the $\mathbb{F}_p$ (not $\mathbb{R}$) coefficient choice is *essential* (note p 30: Smith theory fails over $\mathbb{R}$). That is a concrete, proven instance of a finite-field / torsion-flavored invariant doing work that the rational analog cannot do. The dossier's intuition that the missing object should be torsion-valued rather than rank/trace/volume-valued has a real precedent here.
- **It is a query-complexity result, not a circuit result.** Decision-tree (edge-query) complexity is a different, weaker model than $\mathsf{P/poly}$ circuit size, and evasiveness is about the worst-case query count $\binom{n}{2}$, not about super-polynomial circuit lower bounds. The connection to the leading path (the Williams algorithm-to-lower-bound spine, the $\mathsf{TC}^0$ hinge) is by analogy of *method*, not by direct implication. The barrier discipline must be applied carefully (see discrepancy log).

## What this enables / what remains open

**Maps to research directions:**
- **02 (natural-proofs evasion, the MCSP crux):** This is a template for an evasion mechanism. The topological obstruction is non-natural by construction (it does not produce a large constructive property; it is a single-instance homological certificate). For a BUILDER, the question is whether an analogous "complex of a Boolean function / circuit class" can be built whose non-acyclicity certifies circuit hardness, and whether such a certificate is computable by the fast algorithm the meta-complexity spine wants. This is speculative: nothing in Miller proves anything about circuit size.
- **04 (GCT, multiplicity obstructions):** Both GCT and the evasiveness program look for an *obstruction* (a representation-theoretic multiplicity in GCT; a non-vanishing homology class here) that certifies hardness. Miller's program *succeeds* at the query-complexity level where GCT's occurrence-obstruction route hit a no-go (LEARNINGS finding (7), Burgisser-Ikenmeyer-Panova 2016). The contrast is instructive: torsion/$\mathbb{F}_p$ homology classes are the working invariant here, paralleling the dossier's suspicion (finding (9)) that the missing object should be a torsion invariant rather than a multiplicity or a rational quantity.
- **01 / 03 (circuit and proof complexity):** No direct contribution. Evasiveness is a query lower bound, not a circuit-size or proof-length lower bound. Relevant only as a methodological template.

**Concrete open questions / cruxes this source exposes:**
1. **The Karp conjecture itself remains open.** The best general bound is $n^2/4 - o(n^2)$ (Thm 5.5), improved to $8n^2/25 - o(n^2)$ (Thm 5.7); the conjectured value is $\binom{n}{2}$. The author flags (p 68) that proof difficulty has scaled steeply and a *new basic insight* may be needed. This is a live open problem where the topological method is currently stuck below the target, a possible testing ground for new obstruction ideas.
2. **Coefficient-field dependence (the torsion crux).** Proposition 4.4 needs $\mathbb{F}_p$; the result is false over $\mathbb{R}$. This is exactly the rational-vs-torsion distinction the dossier's missing-object hypothesis turns on. Open: is the essential use of $\mathbb{F}_p$ (Smith theory) the kind of non-algebrizing ingredient strand-3 wants, or does the whole argument relativize/algebrize when lifted to a query model with an oracle? Miller does not address relativization at all; this needs an ADVERSARY barrier-checker pass.
3. **Computability of the obstruction.** The missing-object program wants an invariant a *fast algorithm computes*. The evasiveness obstruction (non-acyclicity of $\Delta_h$) is computed about an exponentially large complex (the simplices are all non-$h$ graphs). Open: is there any setting where the homological certificate is efficiently computable, and would that computation algebrize? This is the precise hinge between "topology certifies hardness" (proven here) and "a fast algorithm certifies hardness without algebrizing" (the dossier's goal).

**Discrepancy log (flagged, not resolved):**
- **No barrier analysis in the source.** Miller never discusses relativization, natural proofs, or algebrization. The project's claim that this is a *non-algebrizing* template is the project's reading, not Miller's. The survey only establishes a non-natural, topology-driven *query* lower bound. Whether the method evades all three barriers in the $\mathsf{P}$-vs-$\mathsf{NP}$-relevant circuit sense is unestablished by this text and should be run through `experiments/_shared/barriers.py` before any deeper investment. I flag this rather than asserting the barrier profile, per SURVEYOR discipline.
- **Model mismatch.** The dossier frames this strand against the Williams spine and the $\mathsf{TC}^0$ hinge (a circuit / approximate-degree setting). Evasiveness lives in the decision-tree (edge-query) model. The analogy is at the level of "obstruction certifies hardness," and the $\mathbb{F}_p$/torsion ingredient, not at the level of model or theorem. Treating this as direct evidence for a circuit lower bound technique would overstate it.
- **"Non-contractible" phrasing.** The focus prompt describes KSS as "monotone graph properties are evasive because the simplicial complex is non-contractible." Miller's precise chain is: nonevasive $\Rightarrow$ collapsible $\Rightarrow$ $\mathbb{F}_p$-acyclic, and the contradiction is driven by the fixed-point theorem on an acyclic complex. Collapsibility is a stronger, combinatorial condition than contractibility, and $\mathbb{F}_p$-acyclicity (homology of a point over $\mathbb{F}_p$) is the exact invariant used, not topological contractibility per se. The "non-contractible" gloss is morally right but the load-bearing notion in the proofs is $\mathbb{F}_p$-acyclicity. Worth keeping precise for any formalization (VERIFIER) work.
